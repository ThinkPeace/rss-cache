To take a break from proselytizing, let’s continue the discussion of ontology: that’s the branch of philosophy that deals with the nature of being. In the previous installment, we showed that it has thought-provoking ideas about time, with interesting tie-ins to cosmology and sci-fi. Today, let’s talk about things that could have been.

That which isn’t real

Similarly to epistemology — the study of knowledge — ontology aims for a degree of rigor in how it talks about the world. It’s accepted that some concepts are too ambiguous or otherwise not conductive to philosophical inquiry, but the goal is to have a reasonably precise way to address the largest possible slice of reality.

Epistemology has shown us that this is worthwhile: codifying the methods of reasoning netted us a comprehensive theory of mathematics, ridding the field of numerous paradoxes and giving the remaining hazards well-defined shapes: the halting problem, Gödel’s incompleteness, and so forth.

Unfortunately, a practically useful theory of existence is a bit more elusive. In the most inclusive sense, “existence” is just the right to be that’s bestowed upon any coherent, logically permissible idea. For example, we may say that the number two exists and the number frombty-nine does not. Alas, if we take this view, we end up with a chaotic menagerie of philosophical entities that don’t actually exist and could never occupy the same physical reality.

Most philosophers agree that concrete, singular objects — for example, your left shoe — have firm ontological standing. At the same time, the shoe isn’t just a clump of atoms; the laws of physics have no discernible opinion on where a shoe begins or what purpose it serves. The object is more usefully modeled as a physical manifestation of an underlying concept; the atoms’ shoeness must be established through observer testimony or through the application of some ad-hoc classification formula.

From there, we must give some consideration to the ontological status of the general concept of footwear. The idea has no specific physical embodiment: there’s no Chaussure des Archives in a vault in Paris. Nevertheless, we can distill the essential, common attributes of boots, flip-flops, and sneakers; it’s what lets us look at a new object and exclaim “a-ha, that’s a shoe”. This collection of properties — a universal — probably ought to exist in the sphere of philosophical discourse, no different from the concept of any particular shoe.

This conclusion, in turn, makes it hard to say no to individual objects that exist in the understanding but aren’t currently instantiated in the physical realm. Such possibilia may include William Shakespeare, who’s no longer with us; or any number of completely imaginary creatures, such as unicorns or the girlfriend-slash-boyfriend of your dreams.

The resulting possibilist ontology is filled to the brim with physically nonexistent beings. To make the idea more palatable, we can sort these non-instantiated entities into hypothetical realms — possible worlds — each still bound by the laws of reason, but allowed to diverge from the state of affairs on planet Earth. This way, the ontological clutter is averted and the unicorns have a place to roam.

The logic of “maybe”

The wrench in the works is that these alternative realities are out of reach for conventional formal logic. This system of reasoning operates on bivalent propositions; if pigs don’t fly, there’s no simple way to add “…but they could!”. In an earlier introduction to the topic, I noted that we can construct counter-to-fact conditionals, but such sentences are always true, with no hope of inspecting the world beyond:

“Another subjective issue crops up with the following statement:

It feels wrong, but there’s nothing we can disprove: the consequent (B) is false, but so is the antecedent (A) it’s gated on. We just don’t like that the sentence limits our imagination: perhaps if pigs could fly, all pumpkins would be donut-shaped? But if the task at hand is to work from true premises toward logical conclusions, and A is not a true premise, why the heck are we trying to form opinions about B? It’s a universe that doesn’t exist; we’re not given the axioms that govern it.”

The missive made sense at the time, but in retrospect, it seems a bit short-sighted; we want to reason about these hypotheticals to see where they lead. Are the unicorns having fun, or have we condemned them to a life of philosophical misery? More pragmatically, we need this to talk about consequentialist morality: it’s hard to opine on the merits of choosing option A without examining the alt-reality of option B.

The solution is modal logic; its basic form, due to Saul Kripke, introduces two new operators: ◊ (“it is possible that…”) and ☐ (“it is necessary that…”). By itself, modal logic doesn’t explain the source of modality — i.e., it doesn’t spell out the mechanism by which some propositions are deemed necessary and others are merely possible. Luckily, to ground it a bit better, we can now lean on the concept of possible worlds: ☐p simply tells us that the proposition must be true in every logically-consistent reality, while ◊p says that there’s at least one possible world in which p is true.

Of course, we’re still leaving a pretty important question unanswered: how does a “possible world” actually look like?

The abstractionist within

The abstractionist answer is probably what you expect: every possible world is a primitive, conceptual entity that represents the way things could have been. It is sometimes modeled as a maximal collection of propositions that describe the totality of the world’s hypothetical state of affairs. In this respect, it is reminiscent of the nebulous, uppercase Truth of reality.

The most unsatisfying aspect of abstractionism is that it dodges the question of how these worlds come to be or what space they occupy. They snap into existence from modal logic — a form of computation — so perhaps they’re akin to numbers: consequences of mathematical theory that, if not actively thought about, simply retreat into the metaphysical void and later emerge unharmed.

But then, imagine Wendell Norton of Bellewood, Kentucky. Wendell is 52 years old; every night, he dreams of frolicking in the fields of Unicorn World 17. Is this world truly on the same footing as Unicorn World 3273, of which no one has ever thought at all? And does the same hold for the lives of the inhabitants of these two realities?…

The Central Finite Curve

This is where David Lewis and other concretists chime in with a resounding “yes”. To the adherents of this philosophy, there’s no qualitative difference between possible worlds and the actual one. The distinction is indexical: your subjective self just happens to find itself in one of many parallel, causally-separated realities. It’s similar to how “now” isn’t a distinguished property of any specific time; it’s just the moment you’re in.

Although concretism may seem ontologically extravagant, it meshes well with the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, as developed Hugh Everett around the same time. Everett’s interpretation posits that the collapse of the quantum wave function that occurs upon measurement actually corresponds to the formation of parallel, decohered realities that account for each possible outcome. The theory was originally not taken seriously, but it has gained more acceptance in the past couple of decades — in part because it deals away with some of the persistent paradoxes of the parent theory.

The main problem with concretism is the apparent collapse of morality: if free will exists, then every morally good decision you make spawns a mirror world in which you’ve taken the evil choice (and vice versa). Since the sum of good and evil appears the same, and since your world has no privileged ontological status, it would seem that it doesn’t matter how you behave. Of course, most judges would disagree.

If you find concretism provocative, here’s a parting shot: if all possible worlds exist, what about impossible ones? These are the realities that contain contradictions or where the laws of logic no longer hold. What stops them from being formed — and could you ever end up in one?…
