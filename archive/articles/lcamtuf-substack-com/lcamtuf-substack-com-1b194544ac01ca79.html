Paradoxes of meaning

In the broadest sense, a “paradox” is a contradiction between two apparent truths; that said, we often use the term when no genuine contradiction exists. We may say something like “the more choice I have, the harder it is to choose”, “the only constant in change”, or “I’m too tired to fall asleep”. These paradoxes are just wordplay: a juxtaposition of concepts that conflict with each other only in the vaguest, most poetic sense. They probably won’t stop a rampaging machine unless your adversary happens to be StonerBot 3000 (with apologies to the YCombinator startup working on that platform as we speak).

Most paradoxes of this type are shallow, but some touch on deeper philosophical mysteries. A well-known example is the paradox of the ship of Theseus. The planks of a wooden ship are gradually replaced as they decay; is a ship in which every element has been replaced still the same, or merely a lookalike?

The question may appear navel-gazey for inanimate objects, but it’s more unsettling for living things. We all have a subjective sense of selfhood — of being in a particular body and not in the bodies of all the other people we meet. Further, we accept that this selfhood doesn’t depend on the continuity of consciousness: a person who wakes up in the morning is the same person who went to sleep.

With these assumptions in mind, consider a sci-fi thought experiment involving a teleportation device that captures a perfect molecular-level image of a living being, instantly dismantles the body into individual atoms, and then ships the atoms for reassembly at the destination. If the continuity of perception is not required for selfhood, it stands to reason that it’s still you who wakes up at the end of the process. It would be difficult to articulate a physical or metaphysical basis to suggest otherwise.

If this teleportation process works as advertised, let’s imagine a shipping mishap that results in some atoms being lost; to bring you back, the recipient needs to toss a gram of locally-sourced carbon into the mix. An atom is an atom; a soul, if it exists, presumably isn’t tethered to any single molecule. It follows that the substitution should be harmless; it’s still you who’s stepping out of the teleport.

But if so, why bother with shipping fees? We just need to transmit the blueprint, discard the original material, and source the atoms locally. Our bodies experience constant molecular churn; what does it matter if it happens gradually or all at once?

Even with this change, it seems logical that the scheme ought to preserve subjective selfhood. But all it takes to shatter the illusion is imagine that the system experiences a glitch: you step into the teleporter and a blueprint is transmitted but the disassembly process fails. Your subjective experience must be that you enter the device, encounter an error message, walk out, and demand a refund. But another being just like you walks out on the receiving side. Clearly, that person is just a clone; your subjective self didn’t magically travel through space and time to hop into a new shell.

But if so, did our teleportation scheme ever work at all — or were we always just murdering people and replacing them with lookalikes?

Paradoxes of deduction

Next to paradoxes of meaning, we have paradoxes of reasoning. In contrast to the earlier category, these puzzles rely on unambiguous semantics and derive a contradiction from the premises of the system of reasoning.

For this class of problems, the old sci-fi trope of a paradox-intolerant AI holds merit: a real contradiction in a formal system of logic can cause the entire system to collapse. I cover this property in an earlier in-depth article, but as a quick recap, let’s assume that p is a stand-in for some sentence that’s simultaneously true and false — that is, we assert the truth of both “p” and “not p”.

Next, we introduce a new sentence “p or q”, where q is the spurious statement we want to prove (e.g., “2 + 2 = 5”). This sentence is true regardless of the truth of q; in formal logic, the disjunction operator (“or”) is satified when p is true, and we know that’s the case.

But we also know that “not p” is true, which makes its negation (“not not p”) false; double negation cancels out, so p is evidently false. We’ve previously used valid, formal reasoning to establish that “p or q” is true. That fact is settled, but we’re now adding the knowledge that p is false. If the already-proved “p or q” statement is true but p is false, we must infer that q (“2 + 2 = 5”) is true.

That said, simply yelling “p ∧ ¬p” at a killer robot may not have the desired effect: the AI is under no compulsion to regard the statement as true. For maximum efficiency, the paradox must be an inescapable consequence of the system’s axioms — that is, the rules that govern the world of machines.

The singularity is unavoidable because division can be thought of as the inverse of multiplication: the a in a = b / c represents the solution to a · c = b. If c = 0, there’s no single, good a to choose from the reals.

Surprising truths

Another prominent class of paradoxes consists of statements that are true but that defy intuition. These statements wouldn’t vex a Spock-like AI, but if your adversary is a model trained on Reddit posts about Pokémon, the odds may be in your favor.

Many problems in this class deal with probability. The most famous example is the Monty Hall problem: imagine you’re on a game show where you need to choose one of three doors. Behind two of these doors, you’ll find only goats. Behind the third door awaits the grand prize. You make a choice, but before the door you’ve chosen is opened, the host unlocks another door to reveal a goat. After that, you’re given one final chance to change your mind and pick the other locked door. Does it matter if you switch?

The instinctive answer is that it doesn’t. The correct answer is that you should switch. In some cases, the host can’t pick the door to open at random, as that would risk revealing the prize. In picking the door with a goat, they reveal a sliver of information to you.

To more properly analyze the scenario, we can look at two cases separately. When you make the initial choice, you have a one-in-three chance of being correct. If you picked the right door — again, a 33% chance — the other two doors must lead to goats. The host can open either of them and you win by sticking to your guns. You’re guaranteed to lose if you switch.

But in 67% of all cases, you initially make the wrong choice. This means that one of the remaining two doors leads to a prize, and the host has no choice but to indirectly point you to it by unlocking the other one. In this scenario, you always lose if you stick to your guns and always win if you switch.

In other words, if we consider all possible scenarios, sticking to your guns limits you to a one-in-three chance; but if you act on the intel, the odds jump to two-in-three.

If probability is not your thing, a different example of a truthful paradox goes like this: imagine you bought 100 pounds of potatoes; for the sake of a mathematical argument, let’s assume that a fresh potato is 99% water by weight. You leave the haul in the sun until the water content drops one percentage point, to 98%. What’s the final weight of the pile? The somewhat surprising answer is 50 lbs.

True contradictions

Of course, to destroy a rampaging sentient computer, we might need more than a mere riddle: we must procure a contradiction that strikes at the heart of the machine’s system of logic, throwing it into an endless loop as it desperately tries to resolve the fault.

In formal systems, the most dependable source of such contradictions is self-reference. A simple and well-known example is the liar paradox: “this sentence is false”, or more properly, p = “p is false”. If we take p as a whole as true, we’re effectively also asserting the truth of the self-referential statement “p is false”. On the flip side, if we take p as false, we’re by extension negating the truth of the nested statement — i.e., we’re saying “p is true”. We’re stuck chasing our own tail!

One possible remedy is to assert that our system of logic is only concerned with statements that can bear a single truth value; a self-contradictory sentence can’t, so it’s not invited to our club. There are complications with this, however. First, if we alter p to read “this sentence can’t bear a truth value”, it would seem that we’re back to square one. In mathematics, the problem of self-reference haunted many of early efforts to formalize the foundations of the field. A particular headache was Russell’s paradox: Bertrand Russell proposed the existence of a set R that contained all the sets that weren’t members of themselves. If R didn’t contain itself, then by definition it should. But if it did include itself, it violated the inclusion criteria and needed to be taken out.

The Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory that underpins most of contemporary mathematics resolved this problem with the principle of restricted comprehension, essentially limiting what the system can say about itself. The rule constrains mathematicians to building new sets only from the sets they already have. They can choose elements by any definable criteria, but they can’t reach into the primordial void: in particular, there can be no sets that contain themselves as elements, or that contain every other set.

Yet, even this seemingly stronger limitation doesn’t truly rid us of mathematical self-reference! For one, in any system of mathematics expressive enough to implement standard arithmetic — and thus, to perform computation — we can construct self-referential logic statements that the system can’t possibly resolve as true or false. A familiar manifestation of this is the halting problem. Let’s say we had a computer function called halts(…) that takes the specification of an arbitrary computer algorithm and then decides its outcome. If so, we could write the following code:

function foo() {
  if (halts(foo)) loop_forever();
}

This program loops forever if the oracle deems to halt, and halts if it’s deemed a non-halting entity. The resulting contradiction tells us such a working algorithmic oracle of this sort can’t exist; to a computer, certain truths about itself are unknowable.

Another manifestation of the issue are Gödel’s incompleteness theorems; the theorems are often interpreted as more profound than the halting problem, but they express substantially the same truth. Gödel invented a method for constructing a self-contradictory statement about numbers, and then showed that no sufficiently expressive system of arithmetic can prove or disprove it without falling apart. I write about Gödel’s proofs and their connection to the halting problem in yet another full-length article.

To knock Gödel off the pedestal a bit more, we may also consider the semi-humorous interesting number paradox. Many natural numbers have interesting properties; for example, 1 is equal to its own factorial, 2 is the only even prime, 4 is the smallest composite number, and so on. Now, suppose there exists the smallest number about which we can’t say anything interesting. If such a number exists, that makes it interesting in its own right! And if that number is interesting, then its successor becomes the smallest uninteresting number, and so on. It would seem that uninteresting numbers can’t exist.

The interesting number problem is closely related to the better-articulated Berry paradox, as captured in the following sentence: “the smallest positive integer not definable in under sixty letters”. This sentence has fewer than sixty letters, so if there exists an integer that can’t be described in sixty letters — and there must be one, you eventually exhaust all syntactic possibilities — then Berry’s sentence seemingly describes it, thus violating the very property the number is supposed to have.

These problems may seem whimsical, but if we express “interestingness” or “definability” in algorithmic terms, the paradoxes are just another incarnation of Gödel’s incompleteness and the halting problem. The numbers we’re talking about can’t be pinpointed by an effective procedure; there are questions about the system of logic that an algorithm can’t resolve. Get the murderbots to ponder an unanswerable riddle and (perhaps) save humanity.
