During our recent visit to Stockholm, we went to the ABBA museum. Although they're obviously a cultural juggernaut, I wouldn't exactly describe myself as a fan of the group. Their songs are great, but I didn't know too much about their outsized influence until I stepped inside. The museum has a fascinating exhibit about the London "Voyage" concert - if you've spent any time in the city you can't have failed to notice all the posters for it. So, as a birthday/Xmas treat for my dad, we picked up some tickets for a chill Sunday Matinée.

Naturally, we dressed up - as did half the audience!

It was astonishing. There's no other word for it. I spent the first ten minutes trying to frantically work out how they did it. They have almost perfectly recreated ABBA on stage. The purpose-build venue seems to have lighting on every surface - I spend half the time gazing around to watch the light and laser show. Similarly, the sound comes from all around you giving it a more "immersive" feel than any other gig I've been to.

But there is something of the "uncanny valley" about the show. One song in particular has the band in "TRON" style outfits and it makes them look like they're in a PS5 cut-scene. At one point, they play in archive film of the band at the Eurovision Song Contest and it blows the CGI out of the water. All of sudden the stiff cheeks, overly smooth skin, and slightly weird lips become apparent - it's like a spell is broken.

The live backing band is incredible - giving it 110% on a dreary autumn afternoon. It seems a pity they're shunted off to one side of the stage. Speaking of which, the ABBA performers are relegated almost to the back of the stage, leaving the front completely unused. Perhaps that's to help preserve the illusion and keep us watching the massive screens?

We managed to get a deal on tickets and paid £75 per seat. You can absolutely see where all the money has been spent - every minute of the show had something incredible to look at. The cost is a lot less than seeing Oasis, but it is still a chunk of change and puts the price very much in the "treat" category of shows. I paid £125 to see the actual Paul McCartney at the O2 whereas here you're only seeing (an admittedly high quality) simulation of the original band. Mind you, London doesn't seem to be running out of ABBA fans, and the show is regularly booked out for months in advance.

Pre-Show and Post-Show

I'm obsessed with how shows treat their audience both before and after the performance. ABBA Voyage is exemplary.

Take a look at their FAQ it is so obviously designed to calm people down who might be nervous about going to a gig. It covers everything from what to wear (and not wear) to exactly what to expect from the event. There's also a comprehensive section on accessibility. I wish more shows were like that!

As befits a modern venue, there's plenty of space and decent toilet provision. The waiting area is generously sized and has plenty of (overpriced) food and drink. The programme is £15 - and that's the cheap one! The souvenir edition is £18 - or buy both for "only" £30. Seats were comfortable and had plenty of leg room.

After the lunchtime show the bars were still open - a rarity for London entertainment venues. It's a short walk to Pudding Mill Lane DLR. The station almost copes with the influx of people heading back to the city. There's a well managed queue and very friendly station staff.
