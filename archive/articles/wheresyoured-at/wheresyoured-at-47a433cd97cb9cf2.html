How Jensen Huang Created A One-Man Financial Mania Using NVIDIA’s Balance Sheet

My name is Jen-Hsun Huang, and for the last thirty-three years, I thought I was the one and only CEO of NVIDIA. I bought an Israeli networking company, sold some GPUs, got my buddy Satya to build a cluster, I saved the stock market, I made some circular deals — never invest in a former cryptocurrency miner — I tried to sell some GPUs to the Chinese, I helped out some of my buddies, all to keep the AI bubble alive…

While the AI bubble is relatively young, the NVIDIA bubble has been inflating for over a decade.

I don’t even mean that derisively! Jensen Huang and NVIDIA were formative to the entire AI bubble, both in creating the infrastructure, demand, and even the underlying research that eventually led to the LLM boom. 

Way back in August 2016, Jensen Huang delivered “the world’s first AI supercomputer” — the DGX-1 — to a small AI lab called OpenAI that had recently raised money from yCombinator’s Sam Altman and Tesla/SpaceX’s Elon Musk. Three months later, OpenAI announced it was moving to Microsoft Azure, training on NVIDIA Pascal GPUs in the earliest days of AI compute.

A few years later, NVIDIA found itself in flux, with a beat on earnings in February 2019 clouded by a decline in both hyperscale and cloud sales, but Jensen Huang was already working on an acquisition that would change everything, agreeing to buy Israeli high-speed networking firm Mellanox that March for $6.9 billion in cash, outbidding both Microsoft (which was already using its tech at Azure) and Intel for the InfiniBand technology that would eventually allow NVIDIA to network together clusters of AI servers. 

Four months later in July 2019, Microsoft would invest a billion dollars into OpenAI “...to build “new Azure AI supercomputing technologies.” Four months later in November 2019, NVIDIA and Microsoft would announce that Azure would now offer a “a new kind of GPU-accelerated supercomputer in the cloud on Microsoft Azure,” networking together up to 800 NVIDIA V100 GPUs using Mellanox’s InfiniBand tech, and the first time I can find where you could rent out massive clusters of AI GPUs. Microsoft and NVIDIA engineers even trained their own conversational AI model called BERT.

The deal itself wouldn’t close until April 2020, and a month later in May, Microsoft and OpenAI would announce that they had built a 10,000 GPU “supercomputer” together, with sources telling The Register that it was likely made up of AMD CPUs and NVIDIA V100 GPUs. 

The same month, NVIDIA would release its A100 GPUs, which remain somewhat of a workhorse for AI Compute, and were a huge commercial success. Another significant milestone came two months later in July 2020, when NVIDIA would publish a blog about how OpenAI researchers had released a paper describing how they’d trained GPT-3 using its Volta-era V100 GPUs (released in 2017). 

In August 2020, NVIDIA’s data center revenue would outpace its gaming revenue for the first time in the company’s history, “aided largely by the Mellanox acquisition” per Holger Mueller of Constellation Research. 

In other words, Jensen Huang and NVIDIA were far more foundational to the AI bubble than Sam Altman or Elon Musk, quite literally buying and building the company and the infrastructure necessary to build large language models. 

While June 2017’s Attention Is All You Need paper absolutely kicked off the transformer-based model boom, NVIDIA and Baidu’s late 2017 research into mixed precision training that allowed model developers to use nearly half as much memory during training without losing accuracy, and also train faster on NVIDIA’s newer chips, a technique that became the standard in the early days of LLMs, shouldn’t be overlooked.

In August 2021, OpenAI would launch Triton, a Python-esque coding language specifically built to make it easy to write code that ran on GPUs that would become foundational to the entire AI boom. Three months later, Microsoft announced that OpenAI’s GPT-3 was now available on an invite-only basis to customers via its new Azure OpenAI Service.

The AI “Boom” Only Made Sense As Long As We Lived in The NVIDIA Bubble

It also has $895 billion in compute commitments, which is the real reason NVIDIA is investing $10 billion at its IPO — Jensen doesn’t care if the chips ever get rented, he cares that somebody has a reason to buy them, or issue debt to the company doing so.

In reality, the “demand” for AI GPUs was manufactured by Jensen Huang and the hype cycle he created through exploiting the desperation of a flailing tech industry.

The industry’s lead cheerleader and House Whip was also its central bank, an evil Michael Clayton figure that loved profiting off of bullying, monopolization, and legal cons that only made sense because he was going to do anything he could to keep you from not paying for AI, which required keeping as many companies alive as possible. 

Anyone else doing this in literally any context would be considered a lunatic. Perhaps Jensen was one too. 

In any case, nobody else has the balance sheet, credit rating, assets, cashflow and psychopathy to do the same. Jensen spent 33 years inflating the NVIDIA Bubble, and it took less than a week for it to collapse. 

I want you to think deeply about why you think there’s “demand” for AI, and how much of that belief is built upon a fantasy concocted by Jensen Huang:

  • There’s massive demand for AI GPUs. Is there? Jensen Huang pushed customers to take billions of dollars’ worth of chips months in advance, with $200 billion or more of them sitting in warehouses thanks to a lack of capacity or power to install them. That “demand” appears to be a result of channel-stuffing, and if it’s not, why the fuck did they have to pay for the chips and take them earlier than they need them?
  • There’s massive demand for AI compute. Is there? The vast majority of AI compute is sold to either Anthropic or OpenAI.   • There’s massive demand for AI tokens. Is there? 80% of Anthropic and OpenAI’s revenues come from 1% of their customers, the vast majority of which are unprofitable AI startups, with the largest purchasers of these tokens — like Perplexity, Cursor, and Reflection AI — all funded by NVIDIA. 

Even Anthropic’s demand appears to be slowing, with growth (per Ramp) trending to around 15% to 25% quarter-over-quarter in Q4 2026, down from 47% in the quarter before and over 140% in the quarter before that. 

One has to wonder how Anthropic — or OpenAI’s — customers will be able to afford those tokens if there’s a generalized sense that the money has run out.

Remember: it’s not just that Jensen invested, but that everybody ultimately relied on the idea that Jensen would always be there if somebody else couldn’t invest. Jensen’s Loss Is Good For Technology, But The Worst Case Scenario For The Tech Industry

As I’ve explained for years, the entire tech industry has become obsessed with growth-at-all-costs thinking — or what I call The Rot Economy — which inevitably dragged it into an era that was defined not by delivered value but by speculation on what might happen if we all put another few trillion dollars into something. 

In reality, buying NVIDIA’s GPUs was always a shitty deal, though nowhere near as bad a deal as renting them.

Hyperscalers won’t share their AI revenues because their AI revenues suck, and they’ve spent $1.4 trillion or more on what appears to be less than $100 billion worth of revenues, with the future theoretical hundreds of billions contingent on hyperscalers spending hundreds of billions more. OpenAI made up 11% of Microsoft’s total revenues in Q1 Fiscal Year 2026, which suggests that Microsoft’s overall revenues are in decline and that its AI revenues aren’t filling the gap outside of an AI lab that lost $10.9 billion in Q3 2026 and raised only $11 billion of the $30 billion it wanted in its latest funding round at $1.25 trillion, which was also lower than expected.

CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, and every neocloud is woefully unprofitable, with the justification being that if they just stopped raising money and finished all of these data centers they might one day have positive margins, somehow. Braindead analysts are now suggesting that “customer prepayments will help instead of debt,” which is contingent on companies that rent AI compute making prepayments, most of whom are unprofitable. 

While hyperscalers could fill the gap, hyperscalers are already in an overbuild situation, mostly driven by Jensen Huang’s pressure campaign. 

Demand for AI services has never, ever made sense, with Anthropic and OpenAI making up 89% of all AI startup revenues and both of them being both unprofitable and agreeing to spend over $1.3 trillion in the next seven years, most of which is contingent on continual funding and debt. We do not know the revenues of effectively any other AI startup, only annualized run rates that tell us very little about the actual financial conditions of the company and the occasional reporting that tell us a dog like Cognition would burn $800 million in 2026 alone. 

Even with the best possible read of those annualized run rates, these startups raise hundreds of millions or billions of dollars’ worth of funding to make tens or hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue, and need to do so every few months due to the remarkable cost of renting NVIDIA GPUs, which despite being multiple generations in do not seem to be getting more efficient for the end customer.

In many ways Jensen Huang created the perfected form of Jack Welch’s GE Capital, manipulating the markets, the media and the banking system into affirming his every decision by making sure that everybody had somebody else to pay or get paid by, even if the entire system was terrifyingly brittle.
