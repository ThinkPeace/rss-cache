At the end of July, Apollo Chief Economist Torsten Slok gave a blunt warning to the Washington Post:

“This AI thing better work out because if it doesn’t ... we’re going to have a problem,” said Torsten Slok, chief economist at investment firm Apollo Global Management.

Today, I’m going to quantify exactly how difficult it will be for the “AI thing” to “work out.”

As I’ve said before:

…everybody wants to make the AI bubble really complex, it’s actually super simple: hundreds of billions of dollars are being invested to make single-digit billions of dollars maybe, some day, if AI data centers actually get built at scale and OpenAI and Anthropic can afford to pay for their compute. 

The theoreticals and hypotheticals of this industry are so powerful that they’ve created one of the (if not the) largest equities bubbles in modern history, all heavily-centralized around the valuations of a handful of names related to the so-called AI trade, which translates to “buying or selling stuff to do with AI GPUs.” 

The AI Trade Is A Global Concentration Risk, With Growth Dependent On Endless Capex Spend and VC-Backed GPU Rentals

This has led to the single-highest inflow of foreign investments into US equities in history — larger than the Dot-Com Bubble, the leadup to the Great Financial Crisis, and even the dying gasps of the Zero Interest Free Era in 2021, per Apollo:

However you might hand-wave away the AI bubble’s economic issues by saying it’s “just like the Dot-Com Bubble” — when the NASDAQ 100 fell 77% from its peak — the consequences are now global, at a scale unseen in history, flowing into both direct stocks and indices.

While the centralization was more severe at the top of the Dot-Com era NASDAQ — with Cisco, Intel and Microsoft accounting for roughly 74% — the total value of the index was a mere $1.82 trillion ($3.58 trillion adjusted for inflation).

There has been a surge in issuance of bonds to finance the construction of data centers for AI computing. This year through Oct. 16, data-center issuers have raised more than $100 billion in the U.S.-dollar bond market, or about 25% of AI-related gross issuance in U.S. currency, according to data from Dealogic and Goldman Sachs credit research analysts. Per the Journal, this debt heavily relies on both the continued value of the data center and critical IT gear and whether the bloody thing actually gets built:

The recent AI data center bonds typically have structures that are different from a company’s bonds. They can be secured by the data center being built, while corporate debt is unsecured. How much security that provides, though, also rests on the completion of the data center, and on the value of the data center to another tenant. Effectively, investors are betting that overall demand for data infrastructure means the data center will be of use to someone, even if the current tenant runs into trouble.

Nomura Says That $60 Billion A Gigawatt Is The Most You Should Spend On A Data Center, And Anthropic Makes About $20 Billion Per Gigawatt, Which Suggests A Massive Mismatch In Data Center Economics

Japanese financial services firm Nomura put out a worrying take a few days ago, emphases mine and from the poster I found it from:

Anthropic hosted a presentation for investors on 15 October. The Financial Times reported sales at Anthropic of $16.8bn in Jul–Sep, versus $11.5bn in Apr–Jun and $4.8bn in Jan–Mar. We have not verified this. We regard ARR at Anthropic as a useful barometer of data center capex, as we think it is currently able to monetize data center capacity better than any other frontier AI lab. For example, (1) assuming that Anthropic has data center capacity of 5GW and ARR of $120bn at end-2026, we estimate that 1GW in compute resources translates into annual sales of $20bn. In addition, (2) if 60% of this revenue is spent on purchasing computing resources, we estimate that it will be able to spend $12bn a year on 1GW of data center capacity, and (3) if data center equipment is depreciated over five years, $60bn per GW represents about the most that can be allocated to data center construction (while noting that data centers also incur operating costs, and that the depreciation scheduled for power infrastructure depreciation is longer than five years). Increased ARR per GW is likely to boost data center spending, and the opposite is likely to hold true too. Assuming that growth in ARR at Anthropic slowed q-q in Jul–Sep, as the Financial Times article suggested, we think this might encourage a slightly more cautious outlook towards data center investment. Moreover, even if rivals were to lose market share to Anthropic, we note that revenues per GW could come under downward pressure across the industry as a whole.

We expect uptake of multi-agent systems (MAS), in which agents collaborate among themselves to execute prompts, and think demand for computing resources will rise once cost-effectiveness has been demonstrated. We think it makes sense to gauge whether the current cost of data center construction is commensurate with the economic benefits that AI models generate by looking at ARR generated by Anthropic and other frontier AI labs, as well as at new services and inventions.

To simmer it down, Nomura is saying based on how much a data center costs both to build and operate, you shouldn’t spend more than $60 billion given the amount of revenue you could hope to generate. 

It’s hard to get a precise measurement of how much one costs. The CEO of IBM said $80 billion a gigawatt at the end of 2025. Foxconn said $47 billion per gigawatt in September 2026. Back in June, Jensen Huang said future data centers could cost $80 billion to $100 billion a gigawatt. It is completely insane we do not have a consensus here.

Nomura also throws cold water on the many people excitedly suggesting that Anthropic could make $50 billion to $75 billion a gigawatt, suggesting instead that the number is around $20 billion. In other words, if Anthropic is paying anything more than $20 million a megawatt, it’s losing money hand over fist just on the compute alone.

Anyway, I sure hope that the people building these data centers aren’t dependent on Anthropic’s revenues…

OpenAI and Anthropic’s Revenue Growth and Fundraising Is Contingent On Unprofitable AI Startups’ (80% Of Revenue) Continually-Increased Token Spend, Which Is Contingent On Venture Capital Funding

Said billions are contingent on the AI labs’ continued revenue growth, and considering that 80% of Anthropic and OpenAI’s enterprise revenues come from loss-making AI startups allowing their customers to burn many times their subscription price in AI tokens (as much as $1,300 on Cognition’s $200-a-month plan, for example), meaning that Anthropic and OpenAI’s revenue growth is contingent on venture capital’s continued ability to fund their customers.

This is one of the core issues with measuring AI demand — that the vast majority of it comes from selling services that do not make any economic sense.

And their spend is contingent on their backers funding them in perpetuity.

That won’t be easy.

Continued Venture Capital Fundraising And Exits Are Hitting Historic Lows, With Investment Heavily Centralized In EBITDA-Negative AI Startups With No M&A or IPO Hopes

This, in turn, is contingent on venture capital’s continued ability to fund raise, and as I discussed in last week’s premium, VC has been on an historic losing streak since 2017, with the median net TVPI (total value put in, as how much you get in return on a per-dollar basis) sitting between 92 cents and a dollar, with 2025 fundraising dropping by 35%, and 2026 fundraising for first-time funds at its lowest rate in over a decade, per Murph Capital, which adds that despite representing only 6% of funds closed, funds of $500 million or more took 78.1% of all capital raised in 2026 to date.

AI startups, which are predominantly unprofitable, unsustainable, and require near-constant flow of venture capital, represented 64% of all venture capital investment in Q3 2026, with 50% of all global venture flowing into AI in 2025. 

As discussed both last year in the Enshittifinancial Crisis and last week’s premium, AI startups represent a devil’s deal for venture — by virtue of being buzzy “AI” companies, their valuations are naturally high, and by virtue of their capital-intensive businesses, they require more rounds of venture capital, with each round increasing valuations to unsustainable heights that make their IP-light, EBITDA-negative businesses impossible for either an IPO or potential acquisition.

In fact, both venture capital and private equity are having real problems selling their investments, with IPOs becoming effectively non-existent as a share of exits for PE, per S&P Global.

Meanwhile, per DealRoom, venture-backed from 2026 seem high, but are inflated by SpaceX’s IPO ($75 billion), the PE-backed take-private of gaming company Electronic Arts ($55 billion), SpaceX’s acquisition of Cursor (all stock, $60 billion), NVIDIA’s acquisition of Hugging Face ($13 billion), and Google’s acquisition of Wiz ($29 billion). 

And since 2015, the $2.7 trillion in venture capital invested has only managed to increase its value by about 1.6x — to around $4.4 trillion in value (not returns), or around a third of what you’d get if you’d have invested in the S&P 500.

In other words, venture capital is not particularly good at creating value, and has, in a period of poor value creation, centralized the majority of its investments in a single industry, with the majority of those investments going to companies related to or powered by Large Language Models that have yet to prove their economic worth.

And in this historically-low period of fundraising and exits, venture capital has invested a historically large amount of money, with the vast majority of it going into one bet: AI.

What’s that phrase? “You should always keep your eggs in one basket, so that you know where they all are”? I think that’s it, right?

Too Many Things Have To Go Perfectly Out For AI To “Work Out”

At this point, the sheer scale of the AI bubble is too large and unwieldy for things, to quote Mr. Slok, “work out,” and yes, I believe we “have a problem.” I say this with no sarcasm, as I think Torsten has done fantastic work posing reasonable, logical questions that nobody seems to want to answer, like “how does this work out?” and “where does the money come from?” even as most media outlets and analysts nod and smile at basically any statement said by an AI lab, hyperscaler, or neocloud.

For everything to “work out,” the current equities surge will have to continue in perpetuity, which requires both hyperscaler AI revenues — which are already depressingly small — and hyperscaler capex to expand infinitely, along with neoclouds, sovereign clouds and AI labs’ vague infrastructure plans to also expand infinitely. 

All the while, there can be no perceptual shift against generative AI, because any further souring of the debt markets will have neoclouds and would-be data center operators paying 12% to 15% in interest to build data centers sometime three years in the future for a customer base that’s pretty much entirely made up of Anthropic and OpenAI. 

As we’ve seen in the last few weeks, AI debt markets can quickly sour even without any bad news, and conditions will only improve in connection to an incredible earnings season for CoreWeave and other AI infrastructure players.

Said “incredible” earnings would require A) data centers to be built and B) said customers to actually pay for them, which roughly translates to “OpenAI and Anthropic’s continued ability to spend more and more money.” Anthropic’s 47% quarter-over-quarter growth rattled the markets because this company has to grow fast enough to cover $413 billion of non-cancellable compute contracts over the next few years, and everybody kind of knows deep down that the bubble can only continue with parabolic growth, which is why everybody freaked out when OpenAI reported a lower annualized run rate (a made up number) than expected, because growth is the only thing keeping this going.

And said growth rate is predominantly based on whether AI startups continue to let their customers spend anywhere from $2 to $10 in tokens per dollar of subscription, which in turn is dependent on venture capitalists’ continued status as the world’s most braggadocious paypigs, and at some point that venture money will run out, much like it did during the Dot Com Bubble.

I didn’t expect this piece to be so sobering, but when I sat down to write it it just kinda flowed out of me how profoundly difficult the AI industry’s future has become. The fundamental disconnection between the perception of AI growth inflating the value of hyperscaler stocks and the worrying fact that we don’t really know exactly how much AI revenue they make means that the S&P 500 and NASDAQ are held up by a kind of childish excitement that will, at some point, turn to existential fear.

Those reflexively responding with “they’ll get bailed out!” neither understand the markets nor the situation at hand. The AI bubble was inflated by hyperscaler revenue growth (without any disclosure of AI revenue) and NVIDIA’s GPU sales, meaning that the moment one of those slows, everybody freaks out. The US government cannot bail out NVIDIA, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, CoreWeave or Meta’s future misses of analyst expectations, nor can it cover every single bankrupt data center, nor can it bail out investor sentiment when it realizes the AI “boom” was yet another tech-based bust. I went into significantly more detail about it in my premium piece about how AI Isn’t Too Big To Fail.

Remember: even with the bank bailout in 2008, the market still crumbled, and that was before we had over $40 trillion in debt, stubborn inflation, and a stock market that has fundamentally disconnected from reality. 

The underlying weakness of the AI trade is that it isn’t really related to anything other than NVIDIA and the hyperscalers’ earnings. 

We don’t know how many GPUs are in operation, we don’t know how much money the hyperscalers make from using them or renting them, we don’t know how many active gigawatts of AI capacity they have, we don’t know their actual customer concentration outside of analyst estimates, we don’t really know how much people pay to rent GPUs, and we don’t even know how much it costs to run a GPU on an hourly basis. 

OpenAI and Anthropic are horrifyingly unprofitable, deeply unstable, and as I’ve discussed, 80% of their revenues come from companies that literally can’t afford to pay them, and yet the markets never react, because this information doesn’t really matter as long as the right numbers keep going up.

This is why Anthropic’s IPO travails are so profoundly dangerous for the AI trade. Investors are now suggesting that it prices its IPO at $1.5 trillion, meaning a significantly lower payoff for investors and much less capital raised on listing, meaning that it will have to take on more and more debt — like its 10% interest chip financing deal with Broadcom and Blackstone — that will provide a brutal education in what a high-burn-rate, negative-EBITDA company really looks like when you remove the layers of annualized run rates and hype.
