As you’ve probably read by now, Jensen Huang, CEO of NVIDIA, has admitted to having an affair with what appears to be a Chinese spy called Alice Huang (no relation), CEO of a company called Megaspeed, and who also goes by the name of Huang Le.

Though far from a household name (though mentioned in an earlier newsletter), Megaspeed is NVIDIA’s largest Southeast Asian customer, per Culper Research, which announced it had taken out a short position on the company in May of this year, citing its belief that NVIDIA’s indirect sales to Mainland China account for significant proportion of its revenue. 

Culper Research’s excellent short report focuses heavily on Megaspeed, a firm with an unusual history and near-empty office in Singapore that, somehow, bought billions of dollars of GPUs in a short amount of time. Said company also has, as I’ll get into, an identical website to a Chinese neocloud that claims to have multiple data centers with advanced NVIDIA chips. 

This was all revealed mere days after NVIDIA’s spokespeople dismissed allegations and called the relationship “completely normal and not unusual” after a New York Times article reported that (Jensen) Huang had been targeted by (Alice) Huang. President Trump had been briefed on the matter around a year ago, but had taken no action as he had, and I quote, “...been wary of disrupting Nvidia’s chip sales, which are helping to drive the economy and the stock market.”

The commerce department, per the Wall Street Journal, had been investigating Megaspeed as a potential chip smuggler since late 2024, with the investigation stalling once it was handed to the Justice Department (which I’ll get to later).

It appears, per the Journal, that the goal was to persuade Huang to use his influence with the highest echelons of American political power to get them to loosen chip export restrictions — though I’ll argue today that the other part of the plan was to use him to funnel sanctioned GPUs directly to China, irrespective of any policy changes in Washington. 

Per The Journal:

Jensen Huang has long argued that the U.S. should loosen the rules that restrict sales of its chips to China. He has said this would continue Beijing’s dependence on U.S. chips and help Nvidia bring in revenue that benefits Americans, rather than spurring China to become self-sufficient.

Huang has now admitted to the affair, though he left out that he’d hired a PR firm known for handling sex scandals around October 2025 (per WIRED):

In Sunday’s memo, Huang wrote: “To my wife and family: I love you. I know an apology cannot undo what I have done. I hope our family will heal and that, over time, I can earn back your trust.”

Additionally, NVIDIA has launched an “independent investigation with a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel,” but has “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised.” 

As I’m about to explain, NVIDIA’s “investigations” don’t carry very much weight. 

As I alluded to earlier, regular Where’s Your Ed At readers heard a little about the strange tale of Megaspeed back in March — a Singapore-based outfit that, according to Bloomberg in December 2025, was a “once-obscure spinoff of a Chinese gaming enterprise [that] evolved into the single largest Southeast Asian buyer of NVIDIA chips,”  and exhibited odd signs that suggest it might be operating as a front for China.

Per Bloomberg, Megaspeed imported goods “worth more than a thousand times its cash balance in 2023,” with two-thirds of its imports being NVIDIA products. 

From what I can piece together — and I’ll get into some detail later in the piece — Megaspeed was originally a Singaporean-registered mobile app developer called 7Road International that rebranded to Megaspeed in March 2023, after it was acquired by Huang Le Limited, making Alice Huang its managing director. At some point it launched a subsidiary called SpeedMatrix which was somehow able to buy about $4.8 billion worth of NVIDIA GPUs.
