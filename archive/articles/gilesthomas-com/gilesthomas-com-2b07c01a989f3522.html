Normalisation is an essential part of LLMs. In the GPT-2 code, we use LayerNorm in a bunch of different places -- in each Transformer block, we do it just before we feed our incoming context vectors into the multi-head attention module, and then again before we feed the result through the FFN:

...and we also do one more round just before we project our context vectors into vocab space via the output head.

We do that to make the model more trainable; it helps to prevent vanishing or exploding gradients, by constraining the vectors going into our attention, feed-forward and output head modules so that they have a reasonably stable set of statistical properties.

And when (as part of my black-box reimplementation of GPT-2 in JAX) I tried training an almost-complete model without LayerNorm, training completely failed after about 100 steps -- the loss went up to the kind of level that I'd expect from an uninitialised model, and stayed there despite another 700 or so training steps. (Later on in this post I'll show results from a similar run.)

So, normalisation is important. But it doesn't have to be LayerNorm specifically -- if you look at the currently-popular open models, a lot of them use something called RMSNorm instead, and I wanted to look into why that might be.

In this post I'll go through the differences between LayerNorm and RMSNorm, and show the results of training a few GPT-2 small style models using each.

The principle

RMSNorm was introduced in a 2019 paper by Biao Zhang and Rico Sennrich 1, which is a surprisingly easy read. They note that most people at the time felt that a critical part of LayerNorm -- which they are explicitly comparing their new system to -- is that it does mean normalisation. But they were less sure that it was the important bit, and felt that scale normalisation was its real benefit.

Let's dig into what that means. Firstly, a refresher on LayerNorm.

LayerNorm

Our normalisation layer in the diagram above is being fed with a bunch of context vectors -- one for each token in each sequence in our batch. LayerNorm deals with each of them separately 2. For each one, it performs two steps:

  • Normalises the context vector so that its mean is zero, and its variance is one.
  • Scales and shifts (aka biases) the resulting normalised vector by trainable amounts.

Let's work through that. We'll go through the normalisation first; say you start with a context vector of

(246)

Its mean is (2+4+6)/3=4. If you subtract that from each element, you get

(−202)

...which has a mean of (−2+0+2)/3=0. So that sorts out the mean part of it. I'll call this "re-centring" below -- hopefully it's obvious why!

To make the variance one, we need to divide each element by the standard deviation (SD) of the whole vector (see my previous LayerNorm post for details as to why).

The SD is the square root of the variance, and the variance is calculated by squaring each item's difference from the mean, adding them all together, and then dividing it by the number of elements 3.

Now, there's a neat trick that you can do here. As we have already set our mean to zero in the previous step, each number in our vector is already exactly equal to its difference from the mean. So we can just square them element-wise, and take the average of that, which will give us our variance:

(−2)2+02+223=83=223

Again, SD is the square root of the variance, so here it's 223≈1.63. Dividing our already-recentred numbers by that gives us a normalised context vector of

(−2/1.63...0/1.63...2/1.63...)=(−1.22...01.22...)

We can sanity-check that the result has a variance of 1 -- remember it's just the average of the elements squared:

(−1.22...)2+02+1.22...23=33=1

So, that's the normalisation part of LayerNorm. We've recentred our context vector on its mean, and made it have a variance of one.

Now that we've normalised our context vector, we need to scale and shift it. Intuitively that feels a bit like undoing the careful work we've done to fix the mean to zero and the variance to one. But it's worth remembering that what we're trying to do with our normalisation is make the results have stable statistical properties; what the ideal specific properties might be is something that the network should be learning. So the first phase gets everything into a well-known state, then the shift/scale goes from there to the shape that the network learns that it actually needs.

Both of these operations are done with trainable vectors of the same size as the context vectors -- that is, the scale vector is demb long, and so is the shift vector. They're applied element-wise. So let's say our scale vector was

(321)

After normalisation, our original context vector was

(−1.22...01.22...)

So we multiply that element-wise by the scale vector, and we get:

(−1.22...×30×21.22...×1)=(−3.66...01.22...)

...and that's the scaling done. Similarly, if our shift vector was

(101112)

...then applying it to the scaled vector would be:

(−3.66...+100+111.22...+12)=(6.34...1113.22...)

...and with that, we've completed our LayerNorm.

But it took quite a lot of calculations; let's list them out:

  • Working out the mean: for a context vector of length demb, we have demb−1 additions, then one division.
  • Recentring on the mean: demb subtractions.
  • Working out the variance: we can use our recentred vector for the differences, so that's done, but we need to get the squares of all of the numbers, which is demb multiplications, add them together using demb−1 additions, and then do one division.
  • Variance to standard deviation: one square root.
  • Dividing it all by the SD: demb divisions.
  • Scaling by the scale vector: demb multiplications
  • Shifting by the shift vector: demb additions

So in total we have, per context vector in our batch:

  • Additions/subtractions: demb−1+demb+demb−1+demb=4demb−2
  • Divisions: 1+1+demb=2+demb
  • Multiplications: demb+demb=2demb
  • Square roots: 1

Let's look at RMSNorm next to see how it compares.

RMSNorm

The idea behind RMSNorm is to try to get away with doing fewer calculations. In particular, we skip working out the mean -- we don't even try to re-centre our context vectors. Instead, we just scale them down to the same length. To do that, we work out an average length for each vector -- specifically, the Root Mean Square (RMS, of course) -- and then divide each number in it by that. That done, we do the same scale trick that we did with LayerNorm, but not the shift -- more about that later.

Let's work through that in numbers again; we can start with the same context vector:

(246)

The mean square of that is, as you might expect, the mean of the squares of all of the numbers in it:

MS=22+42+623=4+16+363=563=1823

The root mean square is the square root of that, which is about 4.32, so we divide through by that:

(2/4.32...4/4.32...6/4.32...)=(0.46...0.92...1.38...)

You might intuitively expect the RMS of that resulting vector to be one, and of course it is; the mean square is:

0.46...2+0.92...2+1.38...23=33=1

...and again, we square root that to get RMS which is again 1.

So you can see that by dividing by the RMS, we're going to re-scale all of our context vectors so that they have a new RMS of one. But their means can be all over the place -- we've not tried to do anything to fix them.

That's our abbreviated normalisation; once that's done, just like with LayerNorm, we apply an elementwise scaling operation by a trainable scale vector -- but we don't shift it. Interestingly, in the original paper, they give equations both with and without the shift -- equation (4), in which they first present it, doesn't have a shift, but equation (5), which they call a general form, does. It's not clear to me which one they actually used in their tests, but it's worth noting (and thanks to GPT-6 Astra for digging this up for me) that the code on GitHub posted by one of the authors has an optional bias that is switched off by default.

Anyway, the fashion these days appears to be to use RMSNorm without a shift, and to me that makes intuitive sense; we've normalised the scale of the values in our context vector via the division by the RMS, so "fixing" that to get desirable statistical properties makes sense. But we didn't normalise the means, so the numbers coming out do not have a stable baseline for us to adjust with a shift. We could add one anyway of course, but there's no intuitive need to add a "new mean" back in to replace an old one that was never removed in the first place. It's a bit fuzzy, perhaps, but it makes some kind of sense.

So let's stick with the popular version without shift: how many calculations do we save by doing RMSNorm?

  • Working out the squares of all of the numbers: demb multiplications.
  • Averaging them to get the mean square: demb−1 additions, then one division.
  • Getting the RMS from that: one square root
  • Dividing all of the numbers by the RMS: demb divisions
  • Scaling by the scale vector: demb multiplications

So that gives us a total of:

  • Additions/subtractions: demb−1
  • Divisions: 1+demb
  • Multiplications: demb+demb=2demb
  • Square roots: 1

In terms of differences with LayerNorm we have:

  • Additions/subtractions: (4demb−2)−(demb−1)=3demb−1 fewer
  • Divisions: (2+demb)−(1+demb)=1 fewer.
  • Multiplications: 2demb−2demb=0 difference
  • Square roots: 1−1=0 difference.

So, it's certainly cheaper -- significantly fewer addition/subtraction operations. The question is, is that saving in training time worth it?

In the paper, they train various models of various sorts (including an early Transformer model) and come to the conclusion that it is; they got similar, or in some cases better, loss with RMSNorm than with their LayerNorm training runs. And, of course, the fact that it's widely used in current open LLMs suggests the same.

Would it work well for my own models? And what kind of performance improvement would I get, if any?

It was time to write some code and train some models.

The code

The PyTorch code for RMSNorm was pretty trivial:

class RMSNorm(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, emb_dim):
        super().__init__()

        self.emb_dim = emb_dim
        self.scale = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(self.emb_dim))
        self.eps = RMSNORM_EPS


    def forward(self, xs):
        squares = xs ** 2
        mean_squares = squares.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        rms = torch.sqrt(self.eps + mean_squares)
        return (xs / rms) * self.scale

Our incoming batch of context vectors is shaped b×n×demb, where b is the batch size and n is the sequence length. In the forward method, just as in the worked example above, we get the square of everything, then work out the mean across that last demb dimension so that we have per-context-vector means, keeping the dimensions stable with that keepdim=True so that we wind up with a b×n×1 result rather than a b×n one.

We then take the square root to work out the RMS, but we add on a tiny ϵ first so that it will never be zero (which would cause a divide-by-zero in the next step). And finally we divide the incoming numbers by that adjusted RMS, multiply by our trainable scaling vector, and we're done.

One thing that is worth noting; to me it would be a bit more natural to add on the ϵ in the division, like this:

        rms = torch.sqrt(mean_squares)
        return (xs / (rms + self.eps)) * self.scale

However, I had the LayerNorm code from the book in front of me as I wrote it, and noticed that it adds on its own ϵ before the square root, like this:

    def forward(self, x):
        mean = x.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        var = x.var(dim=-1, keepdim=True, unbiased=False)
        norm_x = (x - mean) / torch.sqrt(var + self.eps)
        return self.scale * norm_x + self.shift

...so I decided to keep it consistent. When I later looked at the docs for PyTorch's built-in RMSNorm class, I saw that it did the same thing as mine, so it seemed to be the "right" way to do it. The two alternatives have very different behaviour when numbers get close to zero, so it was important to be consistent.

And having mentioned PyTorch's built-in RMSNorm, let's dig into that a bit.

The built-in PyTorch classes

A little while ago, I noticed that by using PyTorch's built-in GELU module rather than the hand-rolled one from the code in Sebastian Raschka's book "Build a Large Language Model (from Scratch)", I got a significant speedup when training on my local machine -- from 21,000 tokens/second to 25,000.

PyTorch also has its own built-in LayerNorm and RMSNorm modules.

Now, while I felt that the book could actually have used the PyTorch GELU, it 100% made sense that it showed you the code for LayerNorm -- after all, "Build a Large Language Model (by Bolting Together Random Bits of PyTorch)" doesn't have the same ring to it. And likewise in this set of experiments I wanted to try running my own RMSNorm.

But given that the point of RMSNorm is meant to be speeding up training, it felt like it would be worth seeing how that speedup might compare to using built-in modules rather than hand-rolling our own. After all, the built-in modules can use fused kernels, and that can be a huge advantage. Rather than doing each of the separate steps in the Python code above by dropping down into CUDA, doing the calculations, and then returning to Python to find out what the next step is, the whole thing can be coded in a single CUDA routine that can be run in one go. Even better, that routine can be optimised as a whole -- stuff that's used frequently can be kept in registers, things can be laid out so that the cache is used efficiently, and so on -- whereas with the Python implementation, everything would need to be in the main VRAM at the end of each step. Definitely a win. My guess is that this is a large part of why the built-in GELU is faster, so I expected serious speedups from using PyTorch's normalisation classes.

That meant that I wanted my code to be able to switch easily between different kinds of normalisation.

Normalisation-switching code

As I mentioned at the start of this post, the original GPT-2 code used LayerNorm in three places:

  • Twice in each Transformers block:
    • before the MHA and
    • before the FFN.
  • At the end, just before the output head.

The code for the Transformers bit looked like this:

class TransformersBlock(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        ...
        self.norm1 = LayerNorm(cfg["emb_dim"])
        self.norm2 = LayerNorm(cfg["emb_dim"])
        ...


    def forward(self, inputs):
        ...
        shortcut = x
        x = self.norm1(x)
        x = self.att(x)

        ...

        shortcut = x
        x = self.norm2(x)
        x, this_block_moe_routing_info = self.ff(x)
        ...

...and the output head bit like this:

class GPTModel(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        ...
        self.final_norm = LayerNorm(cfg["emb_dim"])
        ...

    def forward(self, in_idx):
        ...
        x, moe_routing_info = self.trf_blocks((x, None))
        x = self.final_norm(x)

        logits = self.out_head(x)
        ...

That code is essentially the same as Raschka's from the book, with a couple of (inactive in this post's experiments) tweaks to support mixture-of-experts models.

I wanted a config flag -- in that cfg dictionary -- to control the kind of normalisation we used. It seemed pretty clear that so long as the various classes we wanted to use in place of the existing LayerNorm implementation were compatible with it, we could just swap them in. So I replaced the existing lines that created LayerNorms with:

        self.norm1 = make_norm(cfg)
        self.norm2 = make_norm(cfg)
        ...
        self.final_norm = make_norm(cfg)

...and defined make_norm like this:

def make_norm(cfg):
    norm_type = cfg.get("norm_type", "handrolled_layernorm")
    if norm_type == "handrolled_layernorm":
        return LayerNorm(cfg["emb_dim"])
    elif norm_type == "pytorch_layernorm":
        return nn.LayerNorm(normalized_shape=cfg["emb_dim"], eps=LAYERNORM_EPS)
    elif norm_type == "handrolled_rmsnorm":
        return RMSNorm(cfg["emb_dim"])
    elif norm_type == "pytorch_rmsnorm":
        return nn.RMSNorm(
            normalized_shape=cfg["emb_dim"], eps=RMSNORM_EPS,
            elementwise_affine=True
        )
    elif norm_type == "no_norm":
        return nn.Identity()
    else:
        raise Exception(f"Unknown <!--CODE_BLOCK_15744--> of {norm_type!r}")

That gave us the option for the original "hand-rolled" LayerNorm from Raschka, my own RMSNorm implementation, their respective PyTorch implementations, and a no-normalisation option that I felt would be worth running a test against. The explicit setting of eps for the PyTorch implementations made sure that they would be as similar as possible 4. I set elementwise_affine=True for the PyTorch RMSNorm out of an abundance of caution -- it's the default value anyway.

One thing I was initially concerned with, but which turned out to be a non-issue, was weight initialisation. In many of my tests I've found that the models I've been training can perform significantly better or worse just based on the random weights they get prior to training. If you change which modules make up your model -- say by swapping out a LayerNorm for an RMSNorm -- then if those modules consume different amounts of randomness when they're being created, all subsequently-created modules will get different random number results when their weights are initialised and the results of your tests will be polluted by that.

However, on looking at the code again, I realised that there was nothing to worry about here. Raschka's LayerNorm and my own RMSNorm do not use any randomness, because their trainable parameters are set to ones and zeros -- and the PyTorch implementations do the same. I was actually a little sad about that because it meant that I couldn't use a bit of code I was rather proud of, but it did simplify things. 5

That all seemed to work -- I did short test training runs with each of the settings locally to confirm that loss dropped. It was time to test things out.

The training runs

I was, as usual, going to train models from scratch that were based on GPT-2 small, with two differences:

  • No weight tying.
  • No bias on the QKV matrices.

These models come out at roughly 163M parameters, which is very small, but was hopefully large enough to show interesting results.

For the training runs, I would:

  • Use no dropout.
  • Schedule the learning rate: a warmup over the first 5% from zero to 0.0014, then a cosine decay down to 0.00014.
  • Train for the Chinchilla-optimal ~20 tokens per parameter.

Training models is expensive -- for others I've been doing with models of this scale, it takes about 2 days to train them locally on my RTX 3090, or costs about US$40 if I use DDP to train them on an 8x A100 machine with 40 GiB/GPU (which takes about 2h30m).

So I didn't want to train tons of them, and decided that a sensible minimum set would be:

  • One with the old LayerNorm.
  • One with the PyTorch LayerNorm.
  • One with my hand-rolled RMSNorm.
  • One with PyTorch's RMSNorm.

I also wanted to do a quick test with no normalisation just to confirm that training failed.

I did these all on Lambda -- there were some issues with instance availability, but lambda-manager once again proved its usefulness. I was able to start three instances and spread the training runs across them.

All tests were run with the usual set of commands to make sure that the library versions and so on were the same:

...with CONFIG set to each of the training run configurations I'd created:

  • 8xa100m40-rmsnorm-2-pytorch-layernorm
  • 8xa100m40-rmsnorm-3-hand-rmsnorm
  • 8xa100m40-rmsnorm-4-pytorch-rmsnorm

Each configuration was identical apart from the norm_type, plus a small extra difference for the no-norm test, which (conveniently enough) we'll look at now.

From previous experience, I suspected that it was likely to go bad fast, so I set it to checkpoint and log training loss every 10 global steps instead of every 617. And, indeed, by step 530 it started logging a consistent loss of nan on every training step, so I force-quit it at step 640.

These are really interesting! Let's look at training speed first.

Training speed

What we're meant to be looking into here is the performance characteristics of RMSNorm versus LayerNorm, so let's compare like vs like. The hand-rolled RMSNorm was indeed a bit faster to train than the hand-rolled LayerNorm, but only about 2.5% faster, both in terms of training time and tokens per second. For the runs using the built-in PyTorch modules it was an even closer-run thing -- only 0.5% improvement for RMSNorm.

Without doing tons of extra training runs I can't say safely what the noise level is for this kind of thing, but my gut feeling (based on having trained more than 60 of these models now) is that the 2.5% difference is probably real, while the 0.5% one could well just be noise. In particular, it's worth noting that both of the "hand-rolled" training runs happened on the same instance (by chance), though the PyTorch ones were on different ones, which makes the 2.5% number more trustworthy.

But on to the elephant in the room: the difference between the fused PyTorch implementations and the hand-rolled ones is much bigger. With LayerNorm, it took 11% less time to run. The speedup for RMSNorm was a little smaller, at just less than 10%. That is definitely not noise, and completely eclipses the speedup from switching the kind of normalisation! 6

Now let's take a look at the quality of our models.

Test loss

Again, we'll start with the like vs like comparison. Hand-rolled RMSNorm got a test loss that was 3.601292−3.595336=0.005956 worse than the hand-rolled LayerNorm. That's a tiny difference.

Similarly, the PyTorch RMSNorm got 3.572383−3.571158=0.001225 worse loss than the PyTorch LayerNorm.

I'm tempted to say that those numbers were within the noise. However, I don't have any numbers to back that up -- when I looked into noise previously I did measurements for variations in weight initialisation, and for runs with the same initial weights but different random seeds for the post-model-initialisation steps, but those runs used dropout, which the ones in this experiment did not.

In theory, my training loop should be entirely deterministic; no randomness is used after the initial weight initialisation. A while back I did two training runs on 8x A100 machines with the same config, months apart, and the Safetensors files were identical bit-for-bit.

But what we did here was start with a specific set of initial weights, and then see what happened when we trained them with different normalisation settings. It's certainly conceivable that some initial weights might be "in a better place" for training with LayerNorm while others were in a better place for RMSNorm.

So while the fact that both were a little bit worse with RMSNorm is interesting, I'd really need to train a bunch of different models to get any certainty here. Best to chalk it up to noise for now.

Now, back to that elephant... Once again, the big difference here is not between LayerNorm and RMSNorm, but between hand-rolled and built-in PyTorch implementations. For LayerNorm, the PyTorch version got 3.595336−3.571158=0.024178 lower -- better! -- loss than the hand-rolled one. And for RMSNorm, the difference was 3.601292−3.572383=0.028909.

Those are not super-small differences. Based on the various interventions I tested against the GPT-2 code, that's larger than the improvements I got from switching to full-fat 32-bit rather than using Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP -- at 0.012558), more than I got from adding on gradient clipping (0.013209), and more than adding on bias to the QKV weights (0.022141).

I'd need to do significantly more training runs in order to find out if this was due to some kind of noise, and I'll not spend that time and money now. It feels like there are two possibilities:

  1. It's noise due to numerical differences -- some kind of rounding thing, for example. Imagine a tiny difference right at the start of training -- a single parameter gets updated to 0.11892473 with the hand-rolled code, but to 0.11892474 with the fused code. That slightly changes the results from the next training step, so the gradients are different, and now the model diverges a bit more. As we continue over 33,000 training steps over 3.2B tokens, you can imagine that the models could diverge quite a lot. We're on a choppy ride over the loss landscape, always trying to head downwards, and a small change near the start can drastically change where we end up.
  2. Alternatively: maybe there's something systematic that makes the PyTorch implementations better in this case?

On the one hand, the fact that the results were consistent is interesting. If the PyTorch models had both been better than the hand-rolled ones but by different amounts, then that would be easy to ascribe to chance (I make it a 25% probability, though you could argue for 1/6). But the fact that the improvements were so similar is harder to explain.

On the other hand, a systematic difference would be really weird, at least to me. I'm confident that my hand-rolled implementations match the PyTorch ones in terms of the maths -- not only have I checked and double-checked the code, but the LayerNorm one was written by someone expert in the field, and I've also run them past both Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, both on high thinking levels, to check that there are no differences 7. So while I can't rule errors out, I've done a lot to try to eliminate them.

For now, I think I'll loosely chalk this up to noise too. I've seen weird coincidences crop up before -- regular readers might remember that in my post on LoRE, the loss delta of using it on the input side only, when added to the delta of adding it to the output side only, was almost exactly the same as the delta for adding it to both ends. That repro'd over two separate random seeds and really looked like it was a thing -- only to disappear with a change to a part of the code that should not have affected it if it had been a real additive effect. More Tests Needed.

I think an interesting set of experiments to test that would be not only to try with different random seeds, but also to try with different weight initialisation strategies entirely. What would these results look like if, instead of using PyTorch's default initialisation, we used the one they describe in the GPT-2 paper? (More on that in an upcoming post.)

But back to this experiment: what can the results that we have right now tell us?

Wrapping up

I started this set of experiments with two goals: to understand how RMSNorm works, and to see if I could show similar results to the original paper -- that it gets comparable loss to LayerNorm, with a noticeable saving in training time.

I'm happy with the first part of that -- and hopefully if you're reading this, the "how it works" part is clear for you too!

The loss was, I think, also as expected. Again, I'd need to do a much larger-scale experiment with various starting weights to be sure, but the fact that the difference between RMSNorm and LayerNorm was so small was certainly reassuring.

The training speed was a much less impressive result. With hand-rolled code, the 2.5% speedup looked potentially real, but it pretty much vanished when I switched to PyTorch's built-in classes.

The big difference, of course, was that between PyTorch's built-in classes and the hand-rolled ones. The speedup from using fused kernels for this stuff was not such a big surprise, especially after the similar improvement from using the built-in GELU. It would certainly be interesting to see if using torch.compile narrowed the gap, though.

But the improved loss was definitely interesting. Again, I'd need to do a ton of training runs to see if that was real or noise. It seems most likely that it was noise, but the fact that the effect was positive for PyTorch in both cases, and was by such a similar amount, keeps nagging at me.

One remaining question aside from that, though: RMSNorm wasn't obviously significantly faster, especially if you use fused kernels, and while it wasn't obviously worse than LayerNorm, it didn't appear to be better -- so what explains its popularity in so many open weights models?

The first thing I thought was that maybe it's something that becomes more effective in terms of cost reductions as models get larger. However, having thought it through, I think that exactly the opposite is true -- its effects get even smaller as we scale up.

Remember that -- in terms of calculations -- RMSNorm saved 3demb additions/subtractions for each context vector. That would scale smoothly as demb was increased, and of course the number of RMSNorms would increase proportionally as the model gained more layers. So I think that the absolute compute reduction in terms of calculations saved would scale roughly linearly with each of width and depth.

But other aspects of the model's cost scale more than linearly with width. For example, in GPT-2, our FFNs map from demb to 4demb and back again; that means that the number of calculations scales as O(demb2). More modern implementations use SwiGLU, but I believe that the scaling is of the same order.

And that's not even to mention the fact that modern models have longer context, and while the norms scale linearly with the sequence length (and the RMSNorm savings do too), full attention is O(n2) on that.

So if we're only getting, say, 0.5% improvement for these tiny 163M parameter models with a 1,024 token context length, it would be an even smaller percentagewise improvement for larger ones.

Even if that wasn't the case, the amount is pretty small: DeepSeek V3 was widely reported to have cost US$6M to train (excluding R&D and a bunch of other stuff, but that's actually the correct number to use here). So even if we ignore all of the above and use it directly, that would just be a US$30,000 saving from using RMSNorm. And that's basically an upper bound -- we'd expect it to be significantly less.

Another possibility, though, is that it's an architectural thing. These open models have a bunch of differences to the GPT-2-style code I've been using here. They use RoPE for position encoding, and SwiGLU for the feed-forward networks. Mistral has multiple differences in the attention mechanism. On the face of it, I don't see any obvious reasons why those might speed up RMSNorm, but I can certainly imagine that they might "prefer" one form of normalisation over another, resulting in better loss. So perhaps that's what people have found.

But there are plenty of other possibilities. If people have generally found that it's just as good as LayerNorm, then perhaps it's just a fashion -- one model uses it, then the next one has no particular reason to move back, and so on. And there is a general move towards dropping biases from LLMs (for example, from the QKV weights), and RMSNorm would fit that. And anyway, it is a bit simpler!

Whatever the reason: if RMSNorm is that little bit simpler than LayerNorm, by skipping the recentring aspect of normalisation, is there something even simpler that could work even better? While discussing the subject with Claude Opus 5.5, it mentioned "Transformers without Normalization" 8, which is a really interesting paper -- one of the authors has a neat intro on GitHub. They noticed that the transformations that normalisation layers tend to learn wind up looking a bit like the S-shaped mappings you get from the tanh function, so tried using that instead with a system they called Dynamic Tanh (DyT) -- and got good results!

The first sentence in this post was "Normalisation is an essential part of LLMs". Was that wrong? I suppose that depends on what you mean by "normalisation" -- could you regard their tanh trick as being normalisation of some kind? -- though given that Yann LeCun was a co-author on the paper I should probably be a bit hesitant to disagree with his definition of it ;-)

I think their DyT system is something for a follow-on post, though. For now: RMSNorm seems to be essentially the same in terms of model quality, and maybe slightly faster than LayerNorm for the models I've been playing with, at least if you don't use the fused modules.

But using the fused PyTorch modules instead of hand-rolling our own normalisation is a huge win for speed. The loss improvement that came with it in this experiment is very interesting, but I'd certainly want to do quite a lot of extra experiments before claiming it was real!

For now, I think I'll say that I'm happy that I learned how RMSNorm worked, but I don't think I'll add it in as a standard part of my training runs. And the further experiments can go on the pile with the hundreds of other things I'll look into later. Once I've learned about RoPE, and SwiGLU, and all of the rest of it...

def stable_rng():

  6. One possibility that occurs to me: the fact that the LayerNorm got more of a speedup might be due to an inefficiency in the hand-rolled code having been addressed in the PyTorch implementation. Remember, what we started with was written as educational code to explain how LayerNorm works, so it does this:

            mean = x.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        var = x.var(dim=-1, keepdim=True, unbiased=False)
        norm_x = (x - mean) / torch.sqrt(var + self.eps)
        return self.scale * norm_x + self.shift

    Now, remember that in the explanation of LayerNorm above, I noted that once you've recentred the vector, you can re-use that to work out variance, because the recentred vector is already a list of differences from the mean. So you could rewrite the above more efficiently as:

            mean = x.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        recentered = x - mean
        var = (recentered ** 2).mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        norm_x = recentered / torch.sqrt(var + self.eps)
        return self.scale * norm_x + self.shift

    That would save us two calculations of the mean and the differences from it, so would hopefully speed things up.
