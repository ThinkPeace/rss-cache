Yeast vaccines

More is Different has a post on yeast-based vaccines, describing both why yeast-based vaccines are cool (if not self-evident) and how IRB rules can violate free speech and bodily autonomy make self-experimentation difficult:

Buck submitted an Institutional Review Board (IRB) proposal to gain permission to bring home the yeast and then use them to brew beer for his own consumption. To his surprise, an institutional bioethics committee withdrew the proposal from the submission portal before it could even be considered by the IRB. The committee argued that self-experimentation is strictly forbidden. Buck says he can find no written policies to that effect. The committee also argued that Buck could not eat the yeast strain unless it was first registered with the FDA as an Investigational New Drug.

[…]

Rather than continuing to fight the bioethics committee, Buck went to his home kitchen instead.

I applaud this, but I’m a little confused about what legal theory Buck is using. The National Cancer Institute is in Maryland, and Maryland has a state law that says that all human subjects research must have IRB approval. All, with no exceptions. Perhaps Buck does not live in Maryland, or believes this law is unenforceable and/or unconstitutional.

(Many institutions have policies that say that all human subjects research conducted by employees must have IRB approval, even if those employees do it at home in their free time. But as far as I can tell, the National Cancer Institute only requires this for research done as part of official duties.)

Why are art films so good?

A common theory is that modern literature has shrunk into itself. There was a time when literature overlapped with entertainment and was a major cultural force. But nowadays, to be taken seriously as “literature”, a book is (allegedly) required to be so spare/ambiguous/fragmented that it can only appeal to a tiny slice of the population. Everything else is considered low-class “genre fiction”.

You can arguably see the same phenomena in other places. For example, in the golden era of jazz, jazz was pop music, whereas modern jazz is mostly for jazz nerds. Many would claim something similar is happening with modern architecture, in visual art, and various parts of academia.

But does this theory fit with film? To some degree, yes. I personally think American movies peaked in the 1970s, when there was a strong overlap between “art movies” and “popular movies”. Since then, there’s been a bit of a bifurcation: Anora earned 20× less than Deadpool & Wolverine. Often, Anora and D&W would be shown in completely differnt theaters. And James Patterson novels are not a bad approximation of “Deadpool and Wolverine, except it’s a book”.

Still, I have a question: Why are art movies so good? Sure, some of them suffer from a similar disease as modern literature. (Namely having almost no plot and consisting mostly of long still shots in which no one talks and nothing happens.) But all of the recent winners of the Palme d’Or that I’ve seen are both “true art” and also very entertaining.

(For calibration: Shoplifters, Parasite, Titane, Anatomy of a Fall, Anora.)

What’s the deal? Is there something different about films vs. books that led to this different outcome?

Ultra-processed food

Ultraprocessed foods and obesity: Interpreting the evidence

This is a criticism of RCTs that compare diets with ultra-processed food against those without for weight loss. They argue that the results could be explained by traditional nutritional properties like caloric density, soft/hard food texture, sodium, or saturated fat, rather by food reward/palatability or the effects of processing itself. Worth reading, though I’m not sure the degree to which the people who ran those RCTs would disagree. (What would it even mean for some effects to be caused by “processing itself”?)
