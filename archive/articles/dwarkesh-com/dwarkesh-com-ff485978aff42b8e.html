New podcast episode between Ege Erdil and Daniel Kokotajlo. I thought this was going to be a debate, but they ended up agreeing on a lot more than I expected.

Their main point of disagreement is how likely it is that we get some intelligence explosion that gives us AIs more competent than any human expert before 2029. Ege puts this possibility at around 20%, whereas Daniel thinks it’s higher than 50%.

They agree that there is not a huge difference in probability, and that, in either case, the situation is crazy enough that our civilization really needs to grapple with how the world might be transformed, and we need a plan that actually makes sense.

In particular, we should stop antagonizing China if we think some kind of global treaty will be needed to make superintelligence go well.

There was another important crux about how well we should expect future AIs (that swamp humans in population size and cognitive power) to treat us, and how relevant it is that current AIs, by and large, seem to be pretty nice.

We close off with predictions for 2027. Daniel, of course, expects superintelligence next year. Ege’s median for full automation is 2040, but by next year he expects AIs that can beat any video game on Steam, and which can control robots to get simple physical tasks (like folding laundry) done at roughly human speed.

0:00:00 – Will we have superintelligence by 2029?

Dwarkesh Patel

Today, I’m joined by Ege Erdil and Daniel Kokotajlo. Ege is a researcher at Google DeepMind. Daniel is one of the authors at the AI Futures Project, which published AI 2027 and AI 2040.

I wouldn’t call this exactly a debate, because I hope it’ll be more of a discussion. But there is a set of key disagreements you guys have, which leads one of you, Daniel, to believe that AI is likely to kill us all within the next few years, and Ege, you to believe that will not happen. I feel like a big crux in this conversation is just timelines. How soon do you expect extreme superintelligence?

I’ve articulated, and Paul has articulated in the last episode, why there could be extreme superintelligence soon. Ege, why do you think that’s unlikely?

Ege Erdil

The main difference between me and Daniel, and also between me and Paul, is just how much you believe in the software-only singularity.

This is the idea that you have a fixed pool of compute and a roughly fixed amount of data that is at least not synthetic. Then what happens is that you get better AIs. Those AIs are able to use that pool of resources way more efficiently to make progress on AI research, and you get this feedback loop that at least goes for many orders of magnitude before it fizzles out. Then that improvement is so big that you get, I don’t know, 10 years of progress in six months or whatever, something like this.

I think the evidence we have is not sufficient to give high credence to that. Maybe that’s 10-20% likely to happen over the next three years. That’s probably the biggest disagreement, where Daniel thinks that’s over 50% likely.

Dwarkesh Patel

Why do you think it’s more than 50% likely that there will be extreme superintelligence within the next few years, whereas Ege thinks that’s 10-20% likely?

Daniel Kokotajlo

First of all, I don’t think that we will probably all be dead in a couple years. The thing I would say is, unless we substantially slow down the AI companies, then probably we will lose control and there will be misaligned AIs running the show in a couple years. It seems like we’re getting close to fully automating the AI research process. That would be the main claim. How close? It seems like it’s going to happen in 2027. Maybe not, maybe 2028, maybe 2029, but I would say it’s slightly more likely than not in 2027.

Once you have fully automated the AI research process, things will probably accelerate. More likely than not, they will accelerate a lot, as Ege was describing, although I do think there’s some uncertainty there.

Then I think that a lot of the limitations of current AI systems, such as the data inefficiency or whatever, will melt away relatively quickly as progress accelerates dramatically. You’ll end up with AIs that are able to flexibly learn quickly from all sorts of experience. You might call them top-expert-dominating AIs, perhaps. Top-expert-dominating AIs are effectively superintelligence as far as I’m concerned.

Dwarkesh Patel

Here’s a naive question. We go back five years and we have AI systems that can’t even hold a conversation. Now we have systems that are solving Millennium Prize Problems. They’re pretty good coders, et cetera.

If we just go another five years of progress at this pace, do you think it’s likely that that alone gives us AIs that are competitive with human experts? If we’re looking forward five years, do we even need that much RSI?

Ege Erdil

We might not, but I think it’s unclear. If we don’t get any RSI, my median timeline to everything being automated and humans having nothing to do — they’ve been completely automated — is something like 15-20 years from now. That’s a median timeline. So in the next five years, you still have to give it 20% or something that it could happen. I think that’s a very live possibility.

While there are disagreements about software-only singularity and similar things, in some sense those disagreements don’t matter that much. All I’m saying is, Daniel is saying something is 50-60% likely to happen over the next three years, and I’m saying it’s 20% likely. That question might be interesting. But even if it’s only 20% likely, if the situation is very bad, you should still do something about it.

So even though it’s maybe a larger disagreement numerically, I don’t think it’s a significant disagreement qualitatively.

0:04:31 – Why Ege thinks it’s too early to pause

Daniel Kokotajlo

So the reason why you’re working at Google instead of protesting outside of Anthropic, which is a practical difference between our behavior, is less to do with the probabilities on the different scenarios and more to do with… Well, what actually? I’m curious.

Ege Erdil

Several things. In this particular question of whether a pause is a good idea or not, I’m worried about any plan that involves us acting on risks before we have seen some significant version of the costs in the real world. Right now, the thing people always cite in these discussions, since some number of months ago, is the Hugging Face incident. But I personally think the Hugging Face incident didn’t cost that much and was a pretty small incident in the grand scheme of things. It was much less significant than a plane crash in terms of the cost imposed.

I do see it as analogous to if you have a new technology, like nuclear reactors or aircraft or whatever, and you haven’t actually seen anything significant go wrong, but you have all these theoretical arguments for why things might go wrong, and you’re intervening on the basis of that… I’m just suspicious about that approach in general.

The thing I’m challenging when I say that is the view that the way you get aligned AIs is to have some theoretical breakthrough in alignment. Nothing in machine learning has worked like that. Machine learning is not a field that has large theoretical breakthroughs.

There have been no large theoretical breakthroughs over the past 10 years. All of them have been incremental. You stack this compute multiplier on top of this compute multiplier, and you get this efficiency gain. It’s just very incremental. There are a lot of dimensions that you optimize at once. That’s the way you make them more capable.

So I don’t see why you would assume anything different about the way we would make them more aligned.

Dwarkesh Patel

This is maybe a key disagreement between you guys. Are there tractable interventions that we could make? Specifically, are those interventions more tractable if we get more time to make them, interventions that are likely to increase the value of what these AI systems do and how they treat us?

The AI Futures Project has just put out a pacing scheme that you say will delay the intelligence explosion by a year, which you are expecting in 2027. So you think this would slow it down to 2028?

Daniel Kokotajlo

Something like that. We have uncertainty, but yeah.

Dwarkesh Patel

What changes if we get the intelligence explosion in 2028 rather than 2027?

Daniel Kokotajlo

A couple things. First of all, I think that progress is being made on AI alignment and the science of understanding how AI works and all sorts of things like this. It’s not going fast enough, but it is being made. Each additional year of time that we buy, there’s additional progress, which cuts away at that probability mass of the bad outcomes happening.

The second thing I would say is just general awareness, what you might call AGI-pilling of society and of government. This might actually be the most important thing. Most people just don’t really take this stuff seriously, don’t really understand what’s happening or what’s coming. That seems broadly bad to me. We’d probably be in a better situation if more people in general understood what was coming.

I think that process is happening. It might even be happening exponentially. Every year that goes by, more people learn more, more people pay attention, more people start getting involved.

Ege Erdil

It’s just very difficult without tangible things that the technology does in the world to wake people up to its potential. For example, if we pause at the current level of capabilities, let’s assume we do that. Yes, this process of people waking up continues slowly. But just look at the skepticism in the financial markets about the viability of this technology. There’s huge skepticism. That’s also probably just shared broadly throughout the public.

The public doesn’t really like AI. They don’t really like data centers and stuff. But that’s not because they realize what’s going to happen. They just have a much more myopic distaste for it. If we pause now, then people are still not going to understand what’s at stake. The public conversation about this will still be very poor because it will be operating from bad premises.

There’s an advantage to getting the capabilities to a point where it’s evident to everyone that if we keep going from there on, the world will be totally transformed. Because doing this through ordinary persuasion is extremely difficult.

You might get some kind of pause politically, but it would be supported by factions who don’t actually agree with your worldview. They would support it for some other reason. They don’t like big tech companies. They don’t like the fact that all these companies are constructing all these data centers that the local communities they’re in don’t support. It would be for some other reason and not for the reason that you think is most important.

Daniel Kokotajlo

I technically agree with you, but in spirit disagree. Maybe one way of modeling it is something like: for each new AI capability that appears, there are some rapid updates that people make immediately just upon learning that that capability exists. But then there’s a whole bunch of additional updates that get made as that capability gets more deployed into the world and so forth.

Right now capability progress is moving fast enough that we still haven’t nearly exhausted the gains in awareness that would come from just deploying the current capabilities more. If we had a pause where we continue to deploy the current capabilities but not make them smarter, we would continue to see significant gains in awareness over the course of several years. Then it would taper off after more than that.

I do think that things are going fast enough now that to a first approximation, the wake-up is a function of serial time rather than a function of the capabilities. Although there’s some function of the capabilities.

Another thing I would say too is that a lot of the people who have anti-AI opinions don’t share my worldview and don’t have anti-AI opinions for the same reasons as me. But that’s also changing to a larger extent. We’ve seen multiple senators and congresspeople come out and say, “AI could kill us all,” and, “The AIs could go rogue,” and things like that. That’s mostly happened just in the last year.

So that too is trending in what I would consider the right direction, where people are waking up and understanding the magnitude of the situation more. I don’t think it’s trending fast enough in the right direction. I don’t think that it’s going to be where it needs to be in 12 months, but in 24 months maybe, or in 36 months maybe.

Ege Erdil

I agree with the point that if you pause now, you will still get an increase in awareness for a couple more years. But given the distance between where we are now and where I think people need to get, given the implications of technology, I think that’s just not going to happen if you pause at the current level of capabilities.

It might be enough. You might have the view that the current level of support and current political coalitions or whatever are sufficient to get a pause to happen. Then that has the benefits that you care about, that it slows things down, even though it’s not done with a complete understanding of what’s going to happen and it’s done for different motivations. It’s a defensible position.

I’m just worried in the longer term that if we as a civilization just insist on being irrational about this topic, then we will just make a lot of bad choices, a lot of choices that don’t make that much sense. It’s important that people understand that in some ways, the world we’ve been in is going to no longer exist in some sense.

Even if the outcome is very good for us, if you get Daniel’s ideal outcome or something like that, that world is still completely different from today’s world. People don’t realize. Maybe if they realize, they’ll be like, “Wait, we don’t want that world. That doesn’t fit our preference. We want some other world.”

But if there is never an explicit conversation that has the correct factual premises about what’s going to happen, then we will just not be in control of the situation. Because even though in some sense we are in control, in the sense that all the relevant decisions, at least for a while, are being made by humans, they are not being made as a result of some rational deliberation.

I’m really worried about that. Some random thing is going to happen, and we won’t control what happens. It will not be the output of any process that makes any sense. Then we will have to live with it. That’s very scary.

Daniel Kokotajlo

That’s a reason to go slower in AI development.

Ege Erdil

It’s a reason to go slower after sufficiently many people are aware of the potential of technology.

Daniel Kokotajlo

And you think it’s too early for that?

Ege Erdil

I think it’s too early.

0:13:46 – The adversarial stance on China is dangerous

Daniel Kokotajlo

Then what about, instead of going to a complete pause, going slower? Say we do the thing where we restrict AI R&D compute to 10% or 5% of the company’s compute. This slashes the overall pace of progress by a factor of two or three or something.

Perhaps you would agree then that because you are still making forward progress, you are still going to get the levels of awareness eventually.

Ege Erdil

A couple things about that. First of all, I don’t know how to get international coordination for this. Given what we’re currently doing, it’s going to be very hard because the US is being quite adversarial against China right now, especially with recent events. That just seriously undermines the possibility of getting any coordination. It’s actually very myopic and dangerous. They really have to stop doing that.

Daniel Kokotajlo

One of the nice things about the 10% idea is that it would still leave the US companies with about as much compute on AI R&D as the Chinese companies. Also, because a lot of Chinese progress is just coming from the US, you wouldn’t even need a deal to do this level of pacing.

Ege Erdil

For now, but that’s because China is behind on a bunch of the industrial tech that’s required to produce a lot of compute. For example, they don’t have access to EUV machines and stuff like this. The US has been able to get the Netherlands to successfully export control those and so on. But given China’s rate of economic growth and industrial progress, these are temporary advantages. So it gives us some time.

China is not going to be able to catch up in five years, probably. So if you think five years is enough, then you can get five years with a method like this. But if you think we need 15 years or something like that, then I become more pessimistic.

Dwarkesh Patel

Or let alone some global agreement. In the limit, it is worth discussing. If you really believe we’ll have such powerful AI systems, this depends on what you think the nature of destructive technologies is. But it is plausible that you might need some global treaty on, “Hey, you can’t have superintelligent systems that you’ve deployed to go research mirror life or something.” At some point, there’s not just a race. Unless you think you’re just going to literally outcompete every single country.

Daniel Kokotajlo

I agree that ultimately, if we want to have a good future, we’re probably going to need to make some sort of treaty or deal with China and other countries, something like that.

Ege Erdil

If you’re really worried about that risk, there has to be coordination with them. There’s no way to avoid that. You can try to delay things. You can have a five-year lead. You have five additional years. That buys you something. But if you’re really worried about that risk, they could try to build it in five years. It’s not that long.

Any prospects of coordination with them, I think, are being severely damaged by the policy of the US government towards China, and also, to some extent, of Anthropic towards China.

Daniel Kokotajlo

I agree with that.

Ege Erdil

This is very short-sighted. It’s because people don’t understand the stakes of the technology. The awareness that this is a world-transforming technology that completely changes the condition of civilization is just not there.

We have to figure out a way to deal with it, and there’s no way to adequately deal with it without some kind of coordination. And people are torpedoing the prospects for doing that for some very short-sighted, short-term gain of, “Oh, we get a couple years’ additional lead.” It doesn’t make sense, what people are doing. So I think they’re operating from a very different view of what the technology will be like, which I think is going to have bad consequences.

This is another thing I think is not being discussed, even in communities where people are trying to think of, “How do we win? How do we get the safe AI first?” Gwern had an interesting comment about this, I think on LessWrong, where he asked the question: let’s say the US wins. We get our superintelligence. Then what?

What do we do? Do we do some crazy operation to try to overthrow the Chinese government or something like that? If they think that’s what the US is going to do, what are they going to think of any attempt to try to coordinate with them to get a slowdown? They’re inherently going to be extremely suspicious of any such attempt if they think that is the plan.

0:19:30 – There is no plan, because people are in denial

Dwarkesh Patel

I think people correctly point out that I have too many people on my podcast who have this particular view of AI trajectories, and not only AI trajectories, but the implications of that trajectory. However, it does end up being the case that when you talk to a skeptic, for example, Ege, it seems like you just have slightly different probabilities on things. The people who totally dismiss this altogether, I feel like, don’t have reasonable arguments about why AI capabilities will not continue.

If you take seriously the possibility of minds in data centers that learn like humans and which will swamp us in population, thinking speed, agglomerated experience and expertise, et cetera, no people who take that seriously, at least so far as I’ve found, put less than 10% or less than 5% that things go pretty badly for biological humans.

Ege Erdil

This is something I try to emphasize. By people who don’t have enough context on the situation, or who are not carefully reading what I’m saying, I could be considered this person who disagrees with Daniel, that I give 2-3x less probability to various things compared to Daniel or something like that. That’s actually not that important in some sense.

There are some things for which a 3-4x difference can make a difference. If you think the risk of doom right now is 4% a year compared to 1% a year, that can actually make a difference for whether you think we should pause or not. That’s because you’re comparing it to the 1% per year of people who are dying. Then whether you think the risk per year from AI is higher or lower than that is an important analytical question.

But compared to the people who just don’t take the technology seriously, or who have what I would call magical views about the human mind, which are very widespread, all of those people are just in a different world. They’re not actually engaged with what’s happening.

If they were making predictions over the past few years, or past five years or whatever, they would’ve been very, very, very wrong. All their predictions would’ve been completely wrong. Often they don’t make predictions, so they’re not proven wrong.

Dwarkesh Patel

Actually, should we discuss what you, Daniel, predicted in AI 2027, which was released in the beginning of 2025? What did you predict that the world would look like in late 2026?

Daniel Kokotajlo

The biggest thing that was incorrect was that we said China wakes up in summer of 2026. There has been a lot of wake-up, so to speak, in China. They’ve started talking a lot more about existential risk and alignment and superintelligence and things like that, but not to the same extent that we predicted.

As far as we know, they haven’t gotten the state seriously involved in their AI projects and started funding them more, sharing algorithms and things like that, and merging compute. That’s definitely one thing we got wrong.

Some of the stuff we got right was relatively obvious, like the rise of agents, the rise of coding agents in particular. For early 2026, the title of our chapter there was “Coding Automation,” which coincided very nicely with Claude Code taking off.

Ege Erdil

The revenue projections, if anything, were slightly pessimistic, in the sense that they were lower than what ended up happening. But at the same time, by end of year, the market projections seem like they were too optimistic.

Daniel Kokotajlo

We said 30% or something.

Ege Erdil

Yeah, and if anything, it might end up being down, or only slightly up. A bunch of AI stocks are down, Nvidia’s down, and so on. That’s an interesting thing to also maybe talk about a little bit.

To me it looks like there is a big cleavage in worldviews that’s happening here between people in finance, people in New York, and people in San Francisco, maybe is one way to put it.

People who are viewing these as just ordinary stocks see a lot of business risk. They see a lot of default risk on debts. The business plan doesn’t seem very sustainable. The companies are burning a lot of money. Even Anthropic still has negative margin, or maybe very, very slightly positive margin. So it seems very risky, and they’re like, “Can this really go on for another one or two years?” But here people say, “In one or two years, we might all be dead.” That’s a very big difference in views.

Most people in the world, most people who have a lot of money or whatever, still don’t believe any of this, basically. Not only do they not believe what Daniel is saying, there is just a general complete skepticism of the technology, despite what has already happened.

People still don’t, as far as I can see, just buy this premise. Humans are physical objects. We have some capacities. The brain has some capacities. It’s just a physical object. We have enough resources to reproduce what it does. Then that’s obviously going to have completely transformative implications.

That argument is very straightforward. It doesn’t really hinge on these details about exactly when it happens, but people just don’t believe it.

It seems to me like this very deep-rooted irrationality that is tied to maybe lingering notions of the human soul or whatever. There’s a lot of this going on where people don’t want to confront the reality of what’s happening.

The basic thing is that there is a massive transformation that’s going to happen. And you need to understand it’s going to happen if you want to talk about, “What do we do? What do we change? How do we adapt to it? What is our plan?” We have no plan right now. There’s nothing.

The fact that people will be superseded as the things that produce economic value, that has dramatic implications. People don’t think that’s going to happen, so they’re doing nothing about that either. There’s just a bunch of ways in which, with this transformation of the world, we should be talking about what we’re going to do. And we’re not doing that because people don’t think it’s going to happen.

Dwarkesh Patel

Not only that, but when you do talk about it, there’s just a huge backlash. I think it’s just very psychologically disturbing to take this possibility seriously.

I released this article about the Hugging Face incident where I summarized the story. There was a huge backlash to the fact that I used the word “civilization” in it to describe these agent swarms. At the time I thought to myself, “I would have never guessed that this would have happened. But if I had anticipated it, maybe I could have just avoided using that word, so people could actually read what happened and understand the incident.”

Then I thought to myself, “I don’t think it would have mattered. There would have been something else.” You need to find something so you can’t focus on the story itself. If you do realize the nature of the story — forget about the misalignment, just the nature of the capabilities growth — then one, it’s just not a comfortable thing to think about. Two, it makes a lot of other priorities you might have, a lot of other topics you might want to focus on instead, much less relevant.

With regards to the plan, I’ve been reading these accounts of what has gone wrong in wars in the past. Ege, I know you’ve done a much deeper dive on these kinds of things. But why, for example, did World War I turn into a catastrophe? Why did America send troops into Vietnam? Why did we do Iraq?

What you find in many of these situations is that it was not as if the relevant political leaders got in a room and had a discussion about the pros and cons, the costs and benefits, how things could go wrong, for example, if we invade China, if you’re Japan, or if you give some security guarantee to Belgium, or if we send half a million troops into Vietnam: “What if things escalate in ways we don’t expect? Is that worth the benefits?”

Instead, what has happened in these cases, when people try to analyze why things went wrong, is that there actually never was a clear decision that was made in the first place. There never was an accounting of, “This is what could go wrong. This is what the benefits could be. Do we think it is worth it?”

Ege Erdil

And often the people who try to make that discussion happen get socially punished for it. For example, in Japan, there was a member of parliament, I forgot his name, but during the war with China, he stood up in parliament and was like, “Can someone explain to me what is the point of this? Why are we in this war?” Then they kicked him out of parliament. It’s just such an obvious, rational question. Why are we doing this? What is the point?

The motivation, if you look at that war, for example, was very religious in many ways. “Japan has a civilizing mission to save and liberate East Asia,” or whatever. But what they actually did was totally not that. So it seems like these were not at all decisions that were rationally made. I agree with you. Often these things are not planned. Then of course things end up badly.

For the AI thing broadly, what I would say… There’s this quote from Nietzsche. He wrote, “God is dead, but given the way of man, for many thousands of years there may be caves in which his shadow will be shown, and we have to vanquish his shadow too.”

I think that is what AI is doing. We have all these almost quasi-religious beliefs about the human mind, and all of them are going to be undermined. Some people already realize God is dead in this sense. But his shadow is just the things we believe that are left over from our religious view of humanity. That’s going to have to go away.

0:29:37 – Misalignment itself will slow down RSI

Dwarkesh Patel

Let’s step back. The argument for continuing AI progress now is that you will get better models. Those better models will help you do security work, even formally verifying that your model weights and algorithmic secrets are extremely safe, rewriting the entirety of Slack in a safe, secure way. You will get AIs that are better at doing alignment research. You will be doing alignment research on the models that are closer to the ones that will actually fully automate AI research.

What do you make of the argument that progress at this pace should continue for three or six more months, and you then use that crescendo where all this progress in alignment and security could be concentrated to make sure that superintelligent systems have the values we want them to have?

Daniel Kokotajlo

Sounds like a really dangerous plan to me.

You could make the argument in the past, maybe in 2020. You could say, “We still haven’t figured out how to turn these things into agents. So probably whatever alignment techniques we do on pure predictive language models aren’t going to generalize or teach us that much about how to align AI agents.”

Well, now we have AI agents. Not only do we have AI agents, but we are potentially very close to the singularity, the AI agents that would automate the AI R&D. It’s like, “Okay, now it’s time to start slowing down or stopping.”

I no longer buy the argument that our alignment research isn’t that valuable because our current systems are just too different from future systems. They are very different from superintelligence, that’s for sure. But they’re not very different from the type of AI that will potentially pose a threat, because it’s the type of AI that’s automating the AI research process. Therefore, if it’s misaligned, those misalignments can continue into the future. Also, if it’s just not doing its job very well, if it’s more interested in apparent success than actual success, then it produces alignment solutions that don’t actually work but appear to work.

Dwarkesh Patel

Isn’t another way to say what you’re saying that, even forgetting about the pause, the thing we need right now more than anything is a capable alignment researcher, a capable and trustworthy alignment researcher?

Daniel Kokotajlo
