Free lunches (permalink)

An old joke: "Two economists are walking down the sidewalk and one of them points and says, 'Look, there's a $20 bill, right there on the ground!' The other one is dismissive: 'No, if that was a real $20, someone would have picked it up by now.'"

If that joke leaves you scratching your head, then you've been lucky enough to avoid the neoclassical economist's view of the world as a market whose participants, in aggregate, know everything about what people are buying and selling. In that world if there's a "free lunch" – like a $20 bill on the sidewalk – someone will have already taken it.

Likewise, if there's a seller who's priced their goods at $10 and a buyer who's willing to pay $30, there will be an "arbitrageur" who will be shuttling those $10 goods to those $30 customers and pocketing the difference. Give it enough time and either the seller will raise their price to $30, or the buyer will realize they should only be offering $10.

The market will "clear," with everyone getting everything they need in the most efficient way possible, meaning there's no way to improve anyone's position without making someone else worse off:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_efficiency

When markets don't behave like this, neoclassicals hunt for "distortions" that prevent the market from performing this continuous balancing trick. "Distortion" is best understood here as a euphemism for "democratic intervention." If you've ever gotten excited about a commonsense policy solution to a serious economic problem (like public grocery stores as a means of addressing food deserts and greedflation), only to see it shot down by economists who use a lot of jargon to "prove" that it can't possibly work, you've experienced how well "distortion" doomerism keeps us from having nice things.

The economist's allergy to distortions is curiously selective. When a company uses its monopoly power to capture its regulators, or bind its workers to silence, or to erect barriers to entry that keep competitors at bay, somehow that doesn't qualify as a "distortion." Same goes for the situation in which a dominant retailer (Walmart) and a dominant manufacturer (Pepsi) come to a secret agreement to force everyone else to raise prices a ton so that Walmart can raise its prices by a (smaller) ton and still be "the cheapest":

https://pluralistic.net/2024/08/14/the-price-is-wright/#enforcement-priorities

Economists are in love with the idea of the economy as a self-licking ice-cream cone, a beautiful machine that operates all by itself, processing all the information we inject into it by buying and selling and finding the optimal way to produce more of the things we want best at the lowest prices.

But like any love-drunk suitor, economists habitually overlook the market's glaring imperfections. In the self-correcting market, a company with high margins sends up a flare that alerts other, hungrier companies to swarm it, competing to offer the cheapest price. Likewise any company with lots of customers: those crowds of customers should conjure up armies of clever planners and inventors who find more efficient ways to produce the same goods and services and lure those customers away.

This is the crux of market orthodoxy, the idea that competition lowers margins, goading sellers to offer better goods at better prices. Don't take my word for it, here's Adam Smith:

It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity, but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities, but of their advantages.

If this is true, then the surest sign of a "market distortion" is persistent high margins and persistent high market-share. Any company that dominates its market and charges far more than its costs is a crisp, $20 bill lying right there on the sidewalk for all to see. If no one picks up that $20, then it's a sure bet that there's a "distorter" who is preventing the market from acting on the "information" that there is an opportunity here.

America's business lobby loves distortions, so long as they're the kinds of distortions that let the companies safely entrust their $20 bills to the sidewalk without worrying about having them scooped up by a competitor.

Take Big Tech: this is an industry with such persistently high margins and so many customers that there are multiple firms in the sector that were able to piss away hundreds of billions of dollars by manipulating their stock prices with buybacks and building AI data-centers that can never turn a profit:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/09/06/computer-says-huh/#invisible-handcuffs

If the market was "undistorted" then anyone this rich and this stupid would be in serious trouble. Ask a neoclassical to explain Big Tech's prolonged, morbid wealth and they'll insist that this means that we're the stupid ones, because we can't play 11-dimensional chess like Zuckerberg, which is why we can't understand why it was good to piss away $80b on the metaverse.

But we don't have to look very hard to find another, simpler explanation. Big Tech owes its profits to its monopoly power, which allows it to form cartels that collude to rig prices:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jedi_Blue

Big Tech has so much money that it can capture its regulators, securing favorable laws that make it literally illegal to build competing products, so we're all stuck as hostages of the platforms:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/14/sole-and-despotic/#world-turned-upside-down

An "undistorted" market is one where information is presumed to flow freely among market participants. But the business lobby has secured far-reaching legal protection for the nondisclosure and noncompete "agreements" it crams down the throats of its workers, customers and suppliers, making it literally illegal to warn other people about the defects in both its products and its managers' character:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/06/27/zuckerstreisand-2/#autodisparagement

An "undistorted" market is one in which the state intervenes to enforce contracts. Without that intervention, cheaters can beat trustworthy competitors by making promises they have no intention of keeping. Yet, the business lobby has secured government backing for "binding arbitration" waivers that make it illegal to sue people who cheat you, forcing you to have your case heard by a fake judge who works for the cheater and nearly always rules in their favor (and whose rulings are legally secret, so you can't warn others so they don't get cheated as well):

https://pluralistic.net/2020/04/11/socialized-losses/#justice-restored

"Undistorted" markets are supposed to keep firms lean and energetic, forever on the lookout for new ways to attract the best customers and the best workers with the best deals and the best pay. Decades of lax merger enforcement has produced an economy dominated by fat and lazy cartels who substitute bullying and lock-in for innovation and customer service:

https://www.openmarketsinstitute.org/learn/monopoly-by-the-numbers

The business lobby and their tame economists have invented all kinds of gimmicks to "disprove" your intuition that you're worse off every year thanks to cheaters and their rising profits.

For example, there are plenty of mainstream economists who insist that the wage stagnation that has trapped American workers for decades is a hoax. They point to statistics that show that when you include "transfers," American workers are doing just as well as their foreign counterparts, and in particular, that the American public continues to enjoy healthy levels of "consumption" (buying things).

These are very dodgy stats indeed. Here's how they're calculated: the American consumption figures include the health care received by the American public. That health care includes Medicare payments on behalf of Americans who age out of employer-provided insurance, and Medicaid payments made on behalf of poor Americans. These medical services cost 200-300% more than the same services when provided in other advanced economies (Canada, Germany, the UK, etc).

So when a poor American in Buffalo gets Medicaid dialysis at three times the price of a dialysis session in Toronto, that is counted toward that poor person's consumption levels, as though they had just gone out and spent hundreds of dollars on treats from the Cheesecake Factory and the Apple Store. In reality, they've received an overpriced service (whose costs were absorbed by the public purse). They haven't seen an extra dime to spend on either the necessities of life or a little treat:

https://capitalisnt.com/episodes/can-capitalism-be-popular-FcXPThM6

America spends more on health-care than any other rich nation, and gets less for its money than anyone else. In other words: neoclassical economists use the sky-high monopoly prices set by America's overpriced, underdelivering health care cartel to claim that we've got tons of "real" income, because they factor in the hundreds of billions in public subsidy the feds and the states funnel to these persistently high-margin (and therefore definitively inefficient) businesses.

Get rid of the statistical mirages and a very different America comes into focus: one in which large businesses have fused with a corrupted state that serves as bagman and enforcer for the corporate sector as it rips off workers and consumers.

Remember the decades when tech bros insisted that their "disruption" was all in service to shattering the death grip that legacy industries had on our economy? Uber's early champions insisted that the taxi sector had been "distorted" by overreaching regulation. Once Uber injected "information" about willing riders and willing drivers into the system, the market would clear, pairing drivers and riders in such a way that no one could be made better off without making someone else worse off. Today, Uber uses its lock-in to pay drivers less and less and charge riders more and more – and Uber uses IP law to prevent competitors from making an app that lets drivers automatically shop for better pay and apps that let riders find cheaper rides:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/07/11/your-risk/#my-reward

Uber's "perfect market" is a government-backed monopoly, reliant on the state to block anyone from reverse-engineering its app or unionizing its workers. That fusion of the corporation and the state funnels billions from drivers and riders to investors.

Amazon's founding principle is best summed up in Jeff Bezos's oft-repeated motto, "Your margin is my opportunity." Today, 51-60% of every sale on Amazon's platform is captured by the platform. AWS, Amazon's cloud service, is no longer its most profitable line of business. Today, Amazon's largest source of profit is the $80b it makes every year from charging for search-placement, extracting bribes from sellers to show you items that cost more than the best match for your query:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/04/cheating-at-fraud/#absentee-rentier

That's a lot of margin – and a lot of opportunity.

Same goes for Apple: the company's most profitable line of business is the 30% app tax it levies on every payment made using an iPhone or iPad. Apple makes $100b/year charging a 30% commission on payments that cost the company less than 1% to process. That's a lot of margin.

Then there's the ad-tech duopoly, Meta/Google. These two companies make so much money in an ad system where they represent both buyers and sellers in marketplaces they operate (where they also buy and sell), that they have hundreds of billions left to blow on AI misadventures:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/19/spying-v-context/#unattributed

In other words, Big Tech's sidewalk is covered in $20 bills, which the US government bans American firms from picking up. But that's America. What about the rest of the world?

Well, for 25 years, the US Trade Representative has made it America's business to see to it that every US trading partner enacted laws that protected America's tech companies from competitors. Thus the UK's pavements are littered with 20 pound notes, and the EU's are covered in 20 euro notes:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/01/39c3/#the-new-coalition

The US Trade Rep used both a carrot and a stick to extract these policy concessions from the rest of the world. The carrot was the promise of tariff-free access to the American consumer (the world's richest, shoppingest, most numerous consumer cohort) – the stick was the threat of tariffs.

Today, the stick is broken and the carrot is gone. The American consumer's wallet has been drained. American homeowners groan under multiple mortgages. American renters give 50% of their paycheck to Wall Street slumlords. The US healthcare system has emptied their bank accounts and student debt came for the rest. When American economists have to use the fact that you're too poor to afford overpriced health-care to "prove" that you're rolling in dough, you know the end is nigh. America's growth industries are "financial services" that let you pay for a burrito in four installments – and gambling. The median American worker has $955 saved for retirement:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/04/12/always-great/#our-nhs

Then there's tariffs: as November Kelly says, "Trump inherited a poker game rigged in his favor and flipped the table over anyway because he resented having to pretend to play." The tariffs Trump imposed on every country in the world (even the uninhabited penguin islands) destroyed tariffs' deterrent value:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/12/16/k-shaped-recovery/#disenshittification-nations

America's tech margins are the world's opportunity. It's time to move fast and break (tech's) things. It's time to move fast and break kings. Any economist who truly believes in markets would cheer this on: after all, repealing the "market-distorting" laws the US bound the world to can only increase market efficiency, right? Get rid of trade secrecy, nondisclosure and nondisparagement so that market actors have the information they need to make buy/sell decisions. Get rid of IP laws that block reverse-engineering, scraping and other tactics that facilitate switching to a competitor's service, using your own payment processor, or hijacking and rewriting search results to purge paid placement.

Silicon Valley told us we all have a duty to disrupt sclerotic, top-heavy, lazy industries and treat their margins as our opportunity. When they did it to us, that wasn't progress. If we do it to them, that's not piracy.

It's just business.
