Stop hitting yourself (permalink)

Galbraith famously called conservatism "the search for a superior moral justification for selfishness"; he could just as easily have called it, "the search for a philosophy that pretends that power doesn't exist."

After all, if power doesn't exist, then nearly everything you do is a voluntary choice, which means that this world (in which most of us are very poor and some of us are very rich) is a world where some people are rewarded for making good choices and others are punished for making bad choices. It's not an unfair world; quite the contrary, it's the most fair world of all, a world of just rewards and just punishments.

This power-blindness is everywhere in conservative ideology. Take the conservative movement's hostility to the idea of "systemic" relations. From Margaret Thatcher's declaration that "there is no such thing as society, only individual men and women and their families," to DOGE's targeting of all scientific research into "systemic" effects (including research into systemic cancers), the right hates the idea of coercive systems so much that they seek to deny their very existence.

A world without systemic power is a world in which every one of us is responsible for our own successes (and failures). That's why conservatives are so fond of the "marshmallow test," in which kids were promised two marshmallows (later) if they abstained from eating a single marshmallow (now). Followups showed that the abstainers went on to more professional and social success than the "impulsive" marshmallow eaters, which (supposedly) proved that good decisions determined social outcomes.

But conservatives who cite the marshmallow test rarely mention its methodological errors and replication failures. It turns out that the kids who ate the one marshmallow rather than holding out for two were more likely to be from socially disadvantaged backgrounds, and had long experience of authority figures breaking promises of future rewards. These kids rationally chose to eat the marshmallow now, rather than trust in the promise of two marshmallows later. Far from proving that impulsivity was correlated with poverty, the marshmallow test proves that rich kids inherit their parents' privilege along with their wealth, scoring their home-runs from third base:

https://locusmag.com/feature/cory-doctorow-marshmallow-longtermism/

This power-blindness has been incorporated into economic orthodoxy, through doctrines like "revealed preferences," which is the idea that the most reliable indicator of your true preferences is to be found in what you do, not what you say.

Revealed preferences are an important superior moral justification for selfishness. Mark Zuckerberg can say that for all that we talk about how we'd prefer to have more privacy protections in our lives, we all "choose" to join his service, even though we all know that this means he'll spy on us from asshole to appetite. This may sound right, but only if you omit power from your analysis.

Zuckerberg knows that he can use the "network effects" of his service to draw people in who don't want to be there: for example, if your kid's little league team organizes its car-pools in a Facebook group, you might join rather than see your child excluded from the team. Orthodox economics would call this a revealed preference: you prefer being spied on by Mark Zuckerberg to disappointing your child.

But if you've avoided the self-inflicted neurological injury we call "studying economics" (which leaves you incapable of reasoning about or even perceiving power), you'd call this coercion. Mark Zuckerberg uses the fact that the people you left behind when you emigrated to another country are on Facebook to hold you hostage. He uses the fact that the people who share your rare disease have a Facebook support group to hold you hostage. He uses the fact that your customers or audience are on Facebook to hold you hostage. He uses the fact that you love your friends more than you hate him to hold you hostage:

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/08/facebooks-secret-war-switching-costs

It doesn't have to be this way. The idea that you have to be on Facebook in order to communicate with Facebook users is a bizarre throwback to the 1990s, when you had to join Compuserve to talk to Compuserve users and AOL to talk to AOL users. Having to care which service someone else uses is as backwards as having to care which SIM someone else has in their phone before you call them:

https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/06/save-news-we-need-end-end-web

Facebook is a coercive product. Facebook has hostages, not users. If you rail against your friends' use of Facebook, implying that those friends are weak-willed or foolish, you end up letting Mark Zuckerberg off the hook. Your friends aren't revealing a preference for Facebook, they're revealing Zuckerberg's cruel leverage.

Revealed preferences mean nothing without meaningful choices. When Apple added a one-click opt-out from Facebook spying, 96% of iOS users ticked that box. That's the true revealed preference:

https://pluralistic.net/2022/11/14/luxury-surveillance/#liar-liar

Meaningful choice depends on systemic power relations. Take housing: everyone needs to live somewhere. Strip away rent controls, limitations on corporate landlordism, public housing, and high-density zoning and you'll end up in a world where orthodox economists observe lots of people selling their kidneys to pay the rent and are struck by how many people have a revealed preference for only having one kidney:

https://pluralistic.net/2021/06/06/the-rents-too-damned-high/

Without a theory that incorporates power, everything is a voluntary choice. You aren't being gouged on your rent: you prefer a world in which you can't afford to send your kids to summer camp to a world in which your family is homeless.

True believers in market systems are all over this. They call their ideology "voluntarism," and characterize any state interference into the policies set by property owners and bosses as paternalistic and infantilizing. Who is the government to tell you that you, a grown adult capable of making your own decisions, can't sell your kidney?

This tale of decisions without power-relations is at the root of so many of our worst policies. Take "binding arbitration waivers": a contractual term through which you surrender your right to have your claims heard by a judge. Instead, your disputes will be heard by a corporate arbitrator, usually one hired by the company you're doing business with. These arbitration claims are typically heard in secret, sometimes without you or your representatives having the right to be in the room to argue on your behalf! The arbitrator usually works for the company you're doing business with, and (surprise!) they are far more inclined to rule in their employers' favor than a judge or jury; and when they do find in favor of workers or consumers, they award lower damages than judges or juries.

Arbitration itself is fine. Plenty of divorcing couples opt for arbitration rather than a courtroom, which preserves more of their household wealth to support their families after the split. In Canada, First Nations members often opt into arbitration to have their disputes heard by a tribal elder, whose rulings are guided by their band's values rather than colonial law.

There's also space for arbitration among corporations. Modern corporate arbitration was established by statute and limited to large firms of similar size and power. By opting into arbitration, these companies could streamline contractual disputes: that way, a chip foundry and a chip design firm can resolve their differences in a matter of days, rather than litigating for a decade (by which time the chip design would be obsolete and the machine that it was produced for was scrapped and replaced with a better one).

Neoliberalism's "Law and Economics" movement hated the fact that arbitration was limited to large firms of similar size and power. They wanted to put arbitration into "adhesion contracts." These are the one-sided "agreements" you don't get to negotiate, like the fine-print on employment contracts, website terms of service, hotel check-in forms, car rental paperwork, etc.

This group of neoliberal ideologues have a well-developed playbook for getting the legal changes they desire: they bring a series of carefully designed cases to the courts, working their way up to the Supreme Court, building up an edifice of doctrine and precedent that results in slow but massive legal changes. That's how we got Dobbs, the case that overturned legal abortion in America:

https://www.vox.com/23057223/roe-v-wade-abortion-rights-supreme-court-jim-bopp

The same playbook is underway now to overturn marriage equality:

https://hotspotsmagazine.com/2025/04/11/maga-using-dobbs-playbook-to-go-after-marriage-equality/

And to kill essential press freedoms by making it easier for powerful people to sue and destroy media outlets that report on their crimes and misdeeds:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/03/17/actual-malice/#happy-slapping

That's also the playbook that gave us binding arbitration in adhesion contracts. For decades, Supreme Court Justice Antonin Scalia colluded with the Law and Econ movement to bring cases before the court that would allow him to chip away at protections against binding arbitration, leading to the eventual collapse of nearly all restrictions on these arbitration waivers, all in the name of "voluntarism" and "your right to enter into contracts":

https://brooklynworks.brooklaw.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1443&context=blr

Once binding arbitration was in, the sky was the limit. Other clauses – like gag orders, noncompetes, and "non-disparagement" (which bans you from criticizing a corporation) – could be stacked below an arbitration clause, with the effect that corporations you upset could extract huge monetary damages and injunctions without you ever getting a day in court. Arbitrators' orders have the force of law, which means that any failure on your part to heed the arbitrator's judgments can be sent to a real judge, who can hit you with criminal sanctions (including prison sentences) for refusing the secret, one-sided orders of a fake corporate judge.

It's hard to overstate how far-reaching and grotesque this is. If you want a quick way to understand it, consider the case of Jeffrey Piccolo, the widower of Dr Kanokporn Tangsuan. Tangsuan died at a Walt Disney World restaurant after eating food that she had been falsely assured was free from allergens. When Piccolo brought a suit against Disney over his wife's death, Disney asked a judge to throw it out because Piccolo had once signed up for a free trial of the Disney Plus streaming service, whose clickthrough agreement contained a binding arbitration waiver that covered "all claims" against Disney and its divisions and subsidiaries:

https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2024/08/disney-stops-claiming-disney-terms-require-arbitration-in-allergy-death-case/

Faced with a storm of bad publicity, Disney eventually backed away from this gambit, but the company explicitly reserved the right to have every other case brought by someone who had signed up for Disney Plus (or clicked through any of Disney's other binding arbitration waivers) removed to an arbitrator's secret chambers, no matter what the cause.

For a more complex (and consequential) case, consider Sarah Wynn-Williams, the former Facebook executive turned whistleblower, whose memoir Careless People has seen her dragged into arbitration by Meta, which is seeking over $111m through its arbitrator:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/04/23/zuckerstreisand/#zdgaf

This week, I had the pleasure of hearing Wynn-Williams deliver the annual Orwell Lecture at London's Southbank Centre. She took a huge risk in doing so: Meta takes the position that any time Wynn-Williams appears in public for any reason, she violates the nondisparagement and nondisclosure clauses in her severance agreement, both of which are subject to arbitration, rather than a court proceeding:

https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/news-events/news-events/events/the-orwell-lecture-2026-sarah-wynn-williams/

This absurd arrangement would be funny if it wasn't so horrifying. Last summer, Meta asked their arbitrator to add $50,000 to Wynn-Williams's bill because she stood motionless and silent on a stage at the Hay Festival:

https://web.archive.org/web/20260531175604/https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/may/31/meta-legal-action-forces-facebook-whistleblower-to-stay-silent-at-hay-festival

Binding arbitration is so unfair that it's easy to understate it, slipping into language from the more familiar world of legal abuse. For example, the actor Cate Blanchett gave a stirring introduction to Wynn-Williams's lecture, during which she stated that Meta had used "the courts" to silence Wynn-Williams. That's not true! The courts didn't have anything to do with the gag order: it emanated from a corporate lawyer whose invoices are paid by Meta.

As Wynn-Williams stated during her lecture, Meta secured the gag-order that bound her to silence by petitioning their arbitrator in private, without allowing either her or her lawyer to be present, much less present a defense. As Wynn-Williams said, Meta has designed a private censorship system that makes the UK's notoriously overbroad libel laws look benign by comparison: "In libel, the truth is a defense; for me, the truth was the offense."

This system of private censorship was Wynn-Williams's very fitting Orwell Lecture subject. It was very timely, coming as it did after the rape trial and conviction of Russell Brand. During those proceedings, we learned that Brand's lawyers had forced at least one of his victims to sign a gag order that required her to lie to the police if she was questioned about his crimes:

https://taxpolicy.org.uk/2026/11/07/russell-brand-secret-injunction/

Wynn-Williams told her audience that there is a vast corpus of incriminating evidence against the most powerful, wealthiest people and corporations in our society, hidden through this combination of nondisclosure and non-disparagement, often backstopped by binding arbitration.

Though she didn't say so, I think arbitration is the most important part of this story: courts routinely invalidate contractual terms "as against public policy" (for example, a clause requiring someone to lie to the police).

But there's no guarantee that an arbitrator will consider public policy when ruling on a contract's validity, and (as Wynn-Williams's multi-year saga shows), getting a case removed from an arbitrator's board-room to a court-room is so hard that it might even be impossible, even after Meta's arbitrator forbade Wynn-Williams from giving testimony about Meta's crimes to Congress and the Parliaments of the EU and the UK:

https://thehill.com/policy/technology/6245761-sarah-wynn-williams-meta-gag-order/

The great irony here is that "voluntarism" is the basis for this entire system, from arbitration to gag-orders to non-disparagement. Scalia and his allies would tell you that "freedom" is the reason we can't let Sarah Wynn-Williams tell Parliament about the crimes she witnessed. If we banned nondisclosures and non-disparagement clauses from employer contracts and severance agreements, we'd be crushing Wynn-Williams's right to "freely enter" into a contract.

This is a theory of freedom without a theory of power: it is the apex of the quest for a superior moral justification for selfishness, and it is often dressed up in economic rationale. This "free choice" is how we "inject information into the market," by selecting attractive offers and turning down unattractive ones. This is the mechanism that gives us "price discovery" and creates "demand signals" that cause firms to step up their supply and invest in innovation, resulting in greater prosperity for all.

But as Wynn-Williams said during her lecture: what "freedom" does an ex-employee have to negotiate a severance agreement when they're crying in the front seat of their car next to a box of all the family photos from their (former) office, thinking about their looming mortgage? What "preference" is revealed by their signature on an "agreement" through which they promise not to give Congressional testimony about the crimes they witnessed on the job? Is a preference against foreclosure on their home really a "preference"?

This same worship of the "freedom to contract" underpins the system of noncompetes, by which employees "agree" not to work in their chosen field for years if they quit their jobs (some noncompetes also apply to workers who are fired or laid off!). This, despite the fact that the most successful knowledge industries in human history (Hollywood's movie industry and Silicon Valley's tech industry) both grew up in a state where noncompetes are banned since 1872:

https://pluralistic.net/2021/10/24/the-traitorous-eight-and-the-battle-of-germanium-valley/

The "freedom to contract" is dressed up in language of market efficiency, but at its root, it is about the dominance of the powerful over the powerless. The same people who want unlimited noncompetes also rail against no-fault divorce, contraception and abortion, and in favor of gerrymandering and voter suppression. Their "freedom" is a world of jobs you can't quit, husbands you can't divorce, pregnancies you can't avoid or terminate, and politicians you can't vote out of office:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/09/09/germanium-valley/#i-cant-quit-you

This isn't freedom, it's sadism. Orwell warned us of "a boot stamping on a human face forever," but these guys want a world where we're all being forced to punch ourselves in the face by a bully who shouts "Stop revealing a preference for hitting yourself!"

Revealed preferences is the mob boss who says, "You missed a payment, which means I'm gonna chop off a finger: but because I'm a nice guy, I'm gonna let you decide which finger I take." It's the kidnapper who lets you go so you can get the ransom and then sues you for breach of contract because you didn't come back:

https://www.loweringthebar.net/2011/11/man-sues-couple-he-kidnapped.html

It's Wells Fargo forging your signature while fraudulently opening a bank account in your name, then saying you can't sue them because the paperwork they "signed on your behalf" contained a binding arbitration waiver:

https://www.reuters.com/article/us-wellsfargo-accounts-lawsuit-idUSKBN13J1WX/

This same doctrine of "efficient markets" powered by "voluntarism" says that we should let banks deny loans and hoteliers deny rooms to Black people, because to do otherwise would interfere with sacred "freedom of association." If this is truly the "wrong thing to do," the market will determine that, by bankrupting racist businesses. If those businesses thrive, then racism is "efficient" and must be accepted as part of the price of living in a free society:

https://www.nytimes.com/1987/09/15/opinion/why-judge-bork-is-unacceptable.html

A worship of "freedom" without any consideration for power relations is freedom for the powerful, against the rest of us. It's what Peter Thiel was getting at when he wrote that "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible":

https://www.cato-unbound.org/2009/04/13/peter-thiel/education-libertarian/

The "freedom" Thiel wants is the freedom to coerce us into "agreeing" to sell our silence, our obedience, our wellbeing (and perhaps our kidneys), without the state "paternalistically" deciding that we can't "agree" to these things. He's not wrong: the freedom to sell yourself into slavery is incompatible with the democratic prerogative to outlaw slavery.

These oligarchs dream of a world where the only "freedom" that exists is their freedom to force us to "agree" to any arrangement they can coerce us into accepting. They even want to create countries based on this system, onshore-offshore torture camps where you can sell your kidneys and your privacy and your right to a safe workplace:

https://pluralistic.net/2022/06/14/this-way-to-the-egress/#terra-nullius

Which freedom do we want? A lot of us have been convinced that sadists' freedom to destroy us is somehow essential to our own prosperity. We've absorbed the idea that power relations have no place in our thinking about freedom, that our Facebook-using friends are weak or foolish, not held captive by a tech oligarch who cheats at Settlers of Catan (a fact I learned in Sarah Wynn-Williams' Careless People, despite her "freedom" to be bankrupted for revealing it).
