A permutation rearranges the elements of a list. The number of arrangements, say of a deck of cards, is finite, so eventually you have to arrive at an arrangement you’ve produced before, and history will repeat itself. Along the way, some cards may move in smaller cycles than others.

Example

For example, consider the permutation

p = [5, 6, 2, 1, 0, 8, 3, 4, 9, 7]

that sends i to p(i). Here indices start with 0.

The number 2 is a fixed point: p(2) = 2. So it has a cycle length of 1.

The number 1 is part of a cycle of length 3: p(1) = 6, p(6) = 3, and p(3) = 1.

The longest cycle is [0, 5, 8, 9, 7, 4] which has length 6.

Longest cycles

What happens to the length of cycles as you permute longer lists? Obviously the longest possible cycle increases with the length of the list, but what happens to the average length of the longest cycle?

Let Ln be the length of the longest cycle in a random permutation of n items. You’d expect the average value of Ln, written E[Ln], would increase when n increases. But do they increase at the same rate? Conceivably E[Ln] goes to infinity as n goes to infinity, but at a slower rate, say proportional to √n rather than proportional to n. Let’s make a plot to see.

There’s a lot of variance, but the central tendency looks approximately linear in n. Let’s repeat our experiment, this time creating 100 random permutations of each size and averaging their maximum cycle lengths.

As we’d expect from the central limit theorem, averaging over 100 samples reduced the scatter by about a factor of 10.

It appears that the expected value of the maximum cycle length grows in proportion to n, and in fact the expected value is somewhere around 0.6n. In the example at the top of the post, the longest cycle is exactly 0.6n. I didn’t plan that: the permutation example was chosen at random.
