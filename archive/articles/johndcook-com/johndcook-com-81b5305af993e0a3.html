A couple days ago I wrote about how the force of gravity varies over the surface of an oblate planet. Earth is slightly oblate, i.e., it has an equatorial bulge, and this oblateness has a significant effect on satellite orbits. More on that here.

The fictional planet Mesklin in Hal Clement’s novel Mission of Gravity is extremely oblate due to its rapid rotation, and apparent gravity varies by two orders of magnitude between the equator and the poles. As science fiction authors often do, Clement took some aspect of familiar experience and exaggerated it to make it easier to appreciate.

The gravitational potential of an oblate spheroid as a function of the polar angle θ is proportional to

1 − J2 P2(cos θ)

where P2 is the second Legendre polynomial. There are more terms of the form

J2n P2n(cos θ)

but the most significant term is the one with coefficient J2, hence the J2 effect.

I learned about Legendre polynomials long before I learned about gravity calculations, and so my first reaction when seeing the result above would be that gravitational calculations are another example of places where Legendre polynomials pop up. While this is true, it’s historically backward. Legendre discovered the polynomials that now bear his name because they came up in his gravitational calculations.

It would be more historically informed to say that the polynomials falling out of Legendre’s exploration of gravity have found applications elsewhere, and often for the same reasons: because they fall out of calculations involving radial symmetry. That’s why they appear in E&M, acoustics, and quantum mechanics.

Legendre polynomials also appear in applications that have nothing to do with radial symmetry. For example, the most common form of Gaussian quadrature is based on Legendre polynomials. More on that here.
