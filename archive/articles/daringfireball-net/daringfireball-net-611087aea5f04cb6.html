Via Marcin Wichary, who writes:

Designer Pablo Stanley created a really fun interactive exploration of what it takes to create a seemingly simple “composer” (industry’s jargon for a more sophisticated input field or things like it). [...]

Also, I am burying the lede: it’s a really enjoyable and well-crafted experience — don’t miss the sound and music! — and this unique way of talking about interface details could be inspiring in and of itself.

You can also watch the entire thing as a video with Stanley narrating, but I personally liked the interactive version more.

At first I enjoyed this a lot. I got maybe a quarter of the way through it before I thought it was too long — and then poked around and figured out that I wasn’t even halfway done with it yet. At first, yes, it brings to life just how many details a designer might have to think about. But after a bit it’s just interminable neuroticism. I drew this conclusion before I looked up where Stanley works, which I would have won a bet on. I wound up skipping over half of it, which makes this a rare exception — I almost never link to anything on DF which I skipped through.

At the point where I began wondering if this whole thing was a prank of some sort, all I could think was: this is why platforms need a HIG; why the HIG needs to be thorough, thoughtful, and good; and why designers ought to follow the platform’s HIG. It takes humility to defer to a standard platform reference, and humility is what is lacking in much of modern UI design in general, and Stanley’s interactive essay in particular.

And is it really industry jargon to call these “composers”? It’s a text box. It needs some buttons. It ought to look good. Its functionality ought to be obvious. It shouldn’t be needlessly distracting. Get to work. Build that. Move on. If you call these “composers” you might be in a segment of the industry where the life of a designer is so abstract and proudly navel-gazing that you don’t just lose the forest for the trees, but you lose the trees for endless rumination about the texture of bark. Real designers stay focused on helping users get through a delightful forest.
