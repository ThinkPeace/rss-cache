Ben Thompson at Stratechery:

This led to what I termed Nvidia’s Risky Business: Nvidia increasingly used its cash flow and credit rating to backstop and fund the companies who were buying its chips. Bears called this circular financing; bulls called it a necessary bridge to a future where AI revenue paid down these companies’ debt and drove massive upside. What is undoubtedly true is that this was the sort of risky business that is uniquely possible for a multi-trillion dollar company that is completely controlled by one man, who has made an entire career of pushing the limits and making risky bets — and seeing them pay off.

It’s also a series of bets that are unlikely to continue. The neoclouds should be very nervous about losing their patron, along with their creditors and equity holders. The other problem is that the revenue necessary to make all of this build-out work is something on the order of a trillion dollars industry-wide, and it needs to materialize sooner rather than later; if it falls short, it’s the hyperscalers who will prioritize their own compute first, and they will likely be seen as a safe haven for everyone committing to spending. In that world, Nvidia’s neocloud hedge — absent Huang’s willingness to shore the ecosystem up — is liable to turn into a millstone, with their assets flooding the market even as the supply chain ramp-up brings ever more supply to market.

This Nvidia crisis has made me think a lot about an item I recently posted, purely by coincidence: Charlie Munger’s 2020 take on Elon Musk and Tesla: “My thoughts are two. I would never buy it, and I would never sell it short.”

Munger used “it” in both clauses, but I think for the second one, what he really meant was that he wouldn’t short him. That he wouldn’t want to own Tesla stock (which, even today, trades at an absurd P/E ratio over 400), but he wouldn’t want to bet against Elon Musk.

I think the same thing is true for Nvidia. Nvidia in recent years made a lot of bets that investors normally wouldn’t back; but investors were willing to back Jensen Huang. Now Nvidia, the company, is left holding those risky bets and they don’t have Huang, the man, to make those bets feel like winners. I think investors are already looking at this very differently, and it’s why so many copium addicts are wailing like big babies about bringing Jensen back. Denial is the hardest stage of grief to move past.
